Hobart Freeman & Faith Assembly

Answering issue #1: yes, people died — and the number is worse than a single incident

This article started as a direct answer to a reader question on this site's Issues page: "Who was Hobart Freeman and were people in his church dying due to not taking meds?" This is one of the most thoroughly investigated cases of faith-healing-related deaths in modern U.S. history — a peer-reviewed medical study, extensive contemporaneous journalism, and multiple criminal convictions sit behind it, not just allegation. Where sources disagreed on a specific figure, that's stated rather than picking a number to sound precise.

Who he was

Hobart Edward Freeman (1920–1984) was a former Southern Baptist who converted in 1952, earned a Doctorate of Theology from Grace Theological Seminary in Winona Lake, Indiana, in 1961, and briefly taught Hebrew and Old Testament there before being dismissed over doctrinal differences. He went on to found Faith Assembly, an independent charismatic church based in Wilmot, Indiana, growing out of home Bible studies in the 1960s into a congregation that peaked at roughly 2,000 members locally, with affiliated congregations across Indiana, Ohio, Illinois, Tennessee, and Kentucky.

His teaching on medicine escalated over time — Faith Assembly didn't start out banning doctors outright. Former staff say members were originally told to seek medical care if they lacked the faith for supernatural healing. By the early 1980s, Freeman's doctrine had hardened into an absolute prohibition: he taught that medications were "demonic in nature," that the names of medicines were literally the names of demons, and required members to undergo ritual "deliverance" from any medication they'd ever taken — to the point that members contacted relatives to identify childhood medications specifically so they could be renounced.

The deaths

Yes — and this was studied rigorously, not just alleged. The most citable figure comes from a peer-reviewed study, not a news estimate: Asser, S.M. and Swan, R., "Child Fatalities From Religion-Motivated Medical Neglect," Pediatrics, April 1998, which examined 172 U.S. child deaths (1975–1995) across 23 religious groups in 34 states. Faith Assembly had the highest count of any single group studied: 64 documented child deaths — nearly 30 of them from botched deliveries or inadequate postnatal care. The study's own conclusion: "the number of preventable child fatalities and the associated suffering are substantial and warrant public concern."

That figure only counts children, and only 1975–1995, so it understates the full toll. Contemporaneous journalism in the 1980s reported higher, broader estimates: Fort Wayne's News-Sentinel tallied 52 deaths (children and adults) in a May 1983 investigation, later updated to 88; wire and magazine reporting around Freeman's own death in December 1984 cited estimates ranging from 80–91 deaths across roughly eight states. A separate CDC/Indiana State Board of Health study of Faith Assembly members in just two Indiana counties (1975–1982) found maternal and infant mortality dramatically elevated above the general population — sources report the specific multiplier inconsistently (estimates found range from an 8x to a 100x increase depending on how it's framed), so this site won't cite a single number as precise, but the direction, and the fact that state health officials formally studied and confirmed the excess mortality, is well documented.

Named cases from that era include:

  • Natali Joy Mudd, age 4 (1980) — died of an untreated malignant tumor that grew to the size of her head; her parents were investigated but not prosecuted.
  • Pamela Menne, age 15 (1984) — died of chronic kidney failure a coroner testified was medically treatable. This death triggered Freeman's own indictment, below. Her parents were convicted of reckless homicide in June 1985 and received probation.
  • Joel David Hall, 26 days old — died of pneumonia; his parents were convicted and sentenced to five years in prison (1984).
  • Allyson Bergmann and Kimberly Bergmann/Miller — separate cases resulting in additional reckless-homicide convictions of their parents (1984, 1987).

Multiple other parents were criminally convicted of reckless homicide or neglect of a dependent through the mid-1980s, with sentences ranging from probation to five years.

What happened to Freeman

A Kosciusko County grand jury indicted Freeman himself in October 1984 on three felony counts of aiding and inducing reckless homicide, tied specifically to Pamela Menne's death — the prosecutor's theory being that he was responsible for the church-wide practice he oversaw, even without personally intervening in her case. He was awaiting trial, facing up to 20 years, when he died on December 8, 1984, at age 64. The case died with him.

There's a bitter irony worth stating plainly: Freeman had lifelong effects from childhood polio and walked with a limp his entire adult life rather than seek correction. The autopsy attributed his death to severe cardiovascular disease, mild bronchial pneumonia, and gangrenous leg ulceration; a later account from a former associate pastor described it more directly as death from untreated diabetes — consistent with the gangrene, though that's not the language of the contemporaneous autopsy report itself. Either way, the man who taught that medicine was demonic died of an eminently treatable condition, having refused treatment for it himself.

What changed afterward

Indiana narrowed its "spiritual treatment" religious exemption in its child-neglect statute in the wake of these cases, adding language that the exemption does not apply when a child's life or health is in serious danger — that carve-out persists in Indiana law today. It was not a full repeal, though: Indiana still retains a broader religious-practice exemption for less severe neglect findings, and a 2014 legislative push to eliminate it entirely was reportedly defeated. A follow-up academic study (Spence & Danielson, 1987) found Faith Assembly's maternal and perinatal mortality rates dropped sharply in 1983–85 compared to 1975–82, concluding that public health and legal scrutiny had a measurable protective effect — the prosecutions and press attention actually seem to have saved lives going forward, even though they came too late for the children above.

How this connects to NAR

One clarification worth being precise about: Freeman's ministry (1960s–1984) predates the New Apostolic Reformation as a named movement, and Faith Assembly isn't typically classified as part of it — NAR is the later (1990s–2000s) network associated with C. Peter Wagner's apostolic/prophetic governance framework, a different, if related, strand of the charismatic movement covered in this site's main article. Freeman is better understood as adjacent to the Word of Faith / "positive confession" tradition (Kenneth Hagin, Kenneth Copeland, E.W. Kenyon) — he shared their "healing is promised in the atonement" and positive-confession doctrine, though he also broke sharply and publicly with Hagin and Copeland over a separate Christological dispute. His absolutist, ritualized rejection of all medicine appears to have been a distinctive Faith Assembly extreme rather than confirmed standard teaching across the wider Word of Faith movement — that would need its own separate sourcing before this site would assert it as a general claim rather than one about Freeman specifically.

What does connect directly to this site's throughline: Faith Assembly is the same shape of failure as Tony Alamo's ministry and, in a less extreme form, every case in this site's scandal coverage — a single leader's claimed spiritual authority, with no external body able to check it before it produced documented, preventable harm.
