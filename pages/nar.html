The New Apostolic Reformation

What it teaches, and where it goes wrong

This article takes a position: NAR's central theological claims are wrong, for the reasons laid out below. The overview sections aim to state what NAR actually teaches fairly and accurately before critiquing it. Claims drawn from the author's own firsthand experience inside the charismatic movement are labeled as personal observation, not presented as documented fact.

Overview

"New Apostolic Reformation" (NAR) is a term used to describe a set of theological emphases and organizational trends within global Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, centered on the belief that the New Testament offices of "apostle" and "prophet" are active, functioning roles in the church today — not offices that ended with the first-century apostles.

It is important to note from the outset that NAR is not a denomination, has no central headquarters, and has no membership roll. It is better described as a loose, relational network of independent churches, ministries, and leaders, connected through conferences, apostolic "networks," and personal relationships rather than any shared governing structure. Few, if any, leaders commonly associated with the term use "New Apostolic Reformation" to describe themselves — it is primarily a label applied by scholars, journalists, and critics to describe an observed pattern, which is itself a point of contention among those studying it.

Origin of the term

The phrase is generally attributed to C. Peter Wagner (1930–2016) — no personal site survives him; his institutional legacy continues as Wagner University — a former professor of church growth at Fuller Theological Seminary, who used it from the 1990s onward to describe what he saw as a significant shift in global Christianity: a move away from denominational structures and toward networks led by figures he identified as functioning apostles and prophets. Wagner wrote and spoke extensively promoting this framework as a positive development, framing it as a modern-day restoration of New Testament church government.

Core beliefs and practices commonly associated with the term

  • Restoration of apostles and prophets. The central theological claim: that Ephesians 4:11's offices of apostle and prophet, alongside pastor, teacher, and evangelist, are meant to operate continuously in the church, and that recognized "apostles" carry a governing authority over networks of churches and ministries.
  • The "Seven Mountain Mandate." A framework, often credited in part to Wagner and others in this stream, holding that Christians are called to pursue influence over seven spheres of society: religion, family, education, government, media, arts and entertainment, and business. This is the aspect of the movement that has drawn the most political and journalistic attention, particularly its critics' characterization of it as a form of Christian dominionism — the belief that Christians should actively seek to hold and exercise governing authority over civil society. Proponents typically describe it instead as a call to faithful, influential presence in those spheres, not theocratic control.
  • Strategic-level spiritual warfare. A set of practices — including prayer against what practitioners describe as territorial spirits, "prophetic mapping" of geographic areas, and public acts of "identificational repentance" — that Wagner and others taught as a companion practice to the movement's evangelistic goals.

Notable figures and networks

Leaders and ministries that scholars and journalists have most commonly connected to this stream include Cindy Jacobs (Generals International), Che Ahn (Harvest International Ministry), Lou Engle (TheCall), and Bill Johnson (Bethel Church, Redding, California), among others. It bears repeating that this is an externally-applied grouping — inclusion in journalistic or academic accounts of "NAR" does not necessarily mean a given leader accepts or uses the label, and some have publicly objected to it. A fuller list, with every link independently verified, is on the People & Organizations page.

Public and political attention

The movement drew significantly wider public and media attention following the January 6, 2021 events at the U.S. Capitol, after journalists and researchers documented the presence of imagery, language, and figures associated with this stream of charismatic Christianity — including shofars (ceremonial horns) and explicitly "prophetic" rhetoric about the outcome of the 2020 U.S. election. Religion scholar Matthew D. Taylor's research and book The Violent Take It By Force: The Christian Movement That Is Threatening Our Democracy is among the most detailed treatments connecting specific NAR-associated apostolic networks to that event. Outlets including Right Wing Watch and Rolling Stone ( "How the Christian Right Helped Foment Insurrection", "Meet Dutch Sheets, the Right-Wing Apostle of Christian Nationalism" ) have also published reporting on the movement's political footprint. Where this theology goes wrong

This is the core of this site's disagreement with NAR, laid out point by point.

  • The "apostle" claim doesn't hold up biblically. The New Testament's own text ties the office of apostle to a specific, closed qualification: having personally witnessed the resurrected Christ (Acts 1:21–22, 1 Corinthians 9:1, 1 Corinthians 15:8 — Paul explicitly describing himself as the last one this happened to). NAR's model of "apostle" as an ongoing leadership rank, conferred by other apostles rather than by that qualification, is a different office wearing the same title. A body of cessationist and Reformed scholarship — Douglas Geivett and Holly Pivec's A New Apostolic Reformation? A Biblical Response is the most thorough single treatment — makes this case in detail, and this site agrees with it.
  • Unaccountable authority is the actual practical problem. Once someone is recognized as a functioning "apostle" with governing authority over a network, there is no equivalent to a denomination's elder board, doctrinal review process, or external accountability structure standing over them — accountability tends to run apostle-to-apostle within the same relational network, which is not independent oversight. That structure is exactly the shape that has, repeatedly and well-documented in individual cases across high-control religious groups generally, enabled financial and spiritual abuse to go unchecked for years.
  • The Seven Mountain Mandate collapses the church into a political program. Whatever the intent, teaching that Christians are mandated to seek governing influence over civil institutions — government among them — makes the faith's credibility hostage to a specific political program's success or failure, and gives cover to using "prophecy" to bless partisan outcomes after the fact. Matthew D. Taylor's research connecting specific NAR apostolic networks to the events of January 6, 2021 is the clearest documented case of where that leads in practice, not a hypothetical risk.
  • Unfalsifiable prophecy erodes the ability to hold anyone accountable. When a specific, dated prophetic claim fails to happen, the common response within this stream is reinterpretation ("it was fulfilled spiritually") rather than public correction or a reckoning for the prophet's credibility. A prophetic office with no real cost for being wrong isn't functioning as one.

Defenders of the movement's theology generally respond that continuationism (the belief that all New Testament spiritual gifts and offices remain active) has deep roots across the wider Pentecostal and charismatic tradition, predating Wagner's specific framing by decades, and that the "NAR" label flattens a genuinely diverse set of churches and leaders into a single, more sinister-sounding category than the reality warrants. Those are fair points about the label's precision — they are not, in this site's view, an answer to the biblical-qualification argument above, which doesn't depend on the label at all.
