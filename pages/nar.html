The New Apostolic Reformation

An overview — terminology, beliefs, notable networks, and the public debate around it

This article aims for a neutral, sourced overview. Claims drawn from the author's own firsthand experience inside the charismatic movement are labeled as personal observation, not presented as documented fact.

Overview

"New Apostolic Reformation" (NAR) is a term used to describe a set of theological emphases and organizational trends within global Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, centered on the belief that the New Testament offices of "apostle" and "prophet" are active, functioning roles in the church today — not offices that ended with the first-century apostles.

It is important to note from the outset that NAR is not a denomination, has no central headquarters, and has no membership roll. It is better described as a loose, relational network of independent churches, ministries, and leaders, connected through conferences, apostolic "networks," and personal relationships rather than any shared governing structure. Few, if any, leaders commonly associated with the term use "New Apostolic Reformation" to describe themselves — it is primarily a label applied by scholars, journalists, and critics to describe an observed pattern, which is itself a point of contention among those studying it.

Origin of the term

The phrase is generally attributed to C. Peter Wagner (1930–2016), a former professor of church growth at Fuller Theological Seminary, who used it from the 1990s onward to describe what he saw as a significant shift in global Christianity: a move away from denominational structures and toward networks led by figures he identified as functioning apostles and prophets. Wagner wrote and spoke extensively promoting this framework as a positive development, framing it as a modern-day restoration of New Testament church government.

Core beliefs and practices commonly associated with the term

  • Restoration of apostles and prophets. The central theological claim: that Ephesians 4:11's offices of apostle and prophet, alongside pastor, teacher, and evangelist, are meant to operate continuously in the church, and that recognized "apostles" carry a governing authority over networks of churches and ministries.
  • The "Seven Mountain Mandate." A framework, often credited in part to Wagner and others in this stream, holding that Christians are called to pursue influence over seven spheres of society: religion, family, education, government, media, arts and entertainment, and business. This is the aspect of the movement that has drawn the most political and journalistic attention, particularly its critics' characterization of it as a form of Christian dominionism — the belief that Christians should actively seek to hold and exercise governing authority over civil society. Proponents typically describe it instead as a call to faithful, influential presence in those spheres, not theocratic control.
  • Strategic-level spiritual warfare. A set of practices — including prayer against what practitioners describe as territorial spirits, "prophetic mapping" of geographic areas, and public acts of "identificational repentance" — that Wagner and others taught as a companion practice to the movement's evangelistic goals.

Notable figures and networks

Leaders and ministries that scholars and journalists have most commonly connected to this stream include Cindy Jacobs (Generals International), Che Ahn (Harvest International Ministry), Lou Engle (TheCall), and Bill Johnson (Bethel Church, Redding, California), among others. It bears repeating that this is an externally-applied grouping — inclusion in journalistic or academic accounts of "NAR" does not necessarily mean a given leader accepts or uses the label, and some have publicly objected to it.

Public and political attention

The movement drew significantly wider public and media attention following the January 6, 2021 events at the U.S. Capitol, after journalists and researchers documented the presence of imagery, language, and figures associated with this stream of charismatic Christianity — including shofars (ceremonial horns) and explicitly "prophetic" rhetoric about the outcome of the 2020 U.S. election. Religion scholar Matthew D. Taylor's research and book The Violent Take It By Force: The Christian Movement That Is Threatening Our Democracy is among the most detailed treatments connecting specific NAR-associated apostolic networks to that event. Outlets including Right Wing Watch, Rolling Stone, and Vice have also published reporting on the movement's political footprint.

Criticism and debate

Criticism of the movement comes from more than one direction. Politically-focused critics raise concerns about dominionism and the entanglement of prophetic religious claims with partisan politics. Separately, a body of theological criticism — largely from within cessationist evangelical and Reformed traditions, including authors such as Douglas Geivett and Holly Pivec (A New Apostolic Reformation? A Biblical Response) — argues on doctrinal grounds that the offices of apostle and prophet were never intended to continue past the first century, independent of any political concern.

Defenders of the movement's theology generally respond that continuationism (the belief that all New Testament spiritual gifts and offices remain active) has deep roots across the wider Pentecostal and charismatic tradition, predating Wagner's specific framing by decades, and that the "NAR" label flattens a genuinely diverse set of churches and leaders into a single, more sinister-sounding category than the reality warrants.
