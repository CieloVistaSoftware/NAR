Don Basham & Charles Simpson

Two of the "Fort Lauderdale Five," and how their paths diverged

Biographical, not a theological critique — see The New Apostolic Reformation for that, and Derek Prince for the third of the five covered on this site. Every claim below is sourced; where sources disagreed or a fact couldn't be pinned down, that's flagged rather than smoothed over.

Don Basham (1926–1989)

Don Wilson Basham was born September 17, 1926, in Wichita Falls, Texas. He studied at Midwestern State University and then Phillips University, earning a Bachelor of Divinity from its graduate seminary in Enid, Oklahoma. Raised Baptist, he joined the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) in college and was ordained in 1955.

Basham became known through the 1960s and '70s for a deliverance ministry — teaching and practicing, often alongside Derek Prince, public prayer for people believed to be oppressed by demonic spirits. It brought him real notoriety at the time and, for supporters, was seen as a significant strand of the wider charismatic renewal. In 1970 he joined Prince, Bob Mumford, Ern Baxter, and Charles Simpson in developing the "shepherding" teaching on personal spiritual submission; in 1974 he helped establish Good News Church in Fort Lauderdale with Prince and Mumford, and from 1975 to 1981 edited the group's magazine, New Wine.

He wrote roughly sixteen books, including Face Up with a Miracle (1967), Deliver Us from Evil (1972), and Deliver Us from Temptation (1986). Basham died March 27, 1989 — several months before Bob Mumford's public repentance statement later that year. No reliable source this site could access states a cause of death; that detail is left unstated rather than guessed.

Charles Simpson (1937–2024)

Charles Vernon Simpson was born April 6, 1937, in New Orleans. He attended William Carey College and commuted three hours each way to take classes at New Orleans Baptist Theological Seminary. Ordained a Southern Baptist minister in 1955, he pastored Bayview Heights Baptist Church in Mobile, Alabama, from 1957 to 1971, before receiving what he described as baptism in the Holy Spirit at a storefront church in Pensacola planted by Pastor Ken Sumrall.

Simpson became one of the five Fort Lauderdale teachers based at Christian Growth Ministries, and co-founded and sat on the board of New Wine magazine for seventeen years from its 1969 launch. He went on to found Covenant Church of Mobile (1973), Christ Covenant Church in Houston (1992), and Faith Alive Church in New Braunfels, Texas (2016). His bestselling book was Straight Answers to 21 Honest Questions About Prayer (2013); he also wrote Courageous Living, The Challenge to Care, and Ants, Vines and Churches.

After Christian Growth Ministries dissolved in 1986, Simpson founded and chaired the Fellowship of Covenant Ministries and Churches — confirmed directly against a primary source, Christianity Today's 1990 reporting, not just a secondary summary — continuing the "covenant relationships" model under a new banner. That organization is the direct predecessor of today's Charles Simpson Ministries (csmpublishing.org), now led by his son, Rev. Stephen Simpson. Charles Simpson died at home on February 14, 2024, at age 86, following an illness.

November 1989: one man's repentance, not a joint statement

It's often assumed that the Shepherding movement ended with a joint public apology from its remaining leaders. That's not accurate, and it's worth correcting precisely, because the actual record says something more interesting: Bob Mumford's repentance was his own, individual act — delivered publicly in November 1989, more than five years after he had already quietly stepped back from shepherding teaching in 1984. Contemporary reporting from Christianity Today (March 1990) makes clear the other three surviving leaders did not join him in it:

  • Charles Simpson defended the movement. He did not apologize, and said he could not, as a matter of conscience, repudiate the underlying teaching on covenant relationships.
  • Ern Baxter declined to comment — he was teaching in Australia at the time and never publicly repudiated the movement's doctrine before his death in 1993.
  • Don Basham had already died, eight months earlier, and could not have taken part either way.
  • Derek Prince was not part of the November 1989 event's apology in the same sense — Prince had broken away privately in 1983, and by most accounts what he offered around this period was framed as explanation rather than repentance; see his article for the fuller account, including a further, separate statement he made in 1996.

Secondary reporting (Charisma & Christian Life and Ministries Today, February and January/February 1990 respectively, cited via later retrospective accounts this site could not independently pull from archive) places the apology at a Christian Believers United conference in Ridgecrest, North Carolina, with Mumford saying, in part: "I repent. I was wrong. I ask for forgiveness." He specifically acknowledged that families had been split up and lives "turned upside down" by what he called "unhealthy submission resulting in perverse and unbiblical obedience." That specific venue and the full quoted wording are attributed to those 1990 magazine sources rather than presented as independently verified against a primary scan.

Christian Growth Ministries itself, the Fort Lauderdale organization the five taught under, had already dissolved in 1986, at which point Mumford, Prince, Basham, and Baxter released their disciples from the formal "covenant relationships" — three years before Mumford's public apology, and a detail that further complicates any "unified movement, unified ending" narrative.
