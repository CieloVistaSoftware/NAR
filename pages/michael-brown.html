Michael Brown

He ran the accountability process against Todd Bentley. Then his own hand-picked team overrode a finding against him.

This article isn't a straightforward villain piece, because Brown isn't a straightforward villain figure — he's a genuinely complicated one, and forcing him into the same shape as the other entries on this site would misrepresent him. He's a real defender of accountability within the charismatic movement in one documented case, and the subject of a contested one himself in another. Both are true, and both are covered here as precisely as the sourcing allows.

Who he is

Dr. Michael L. Brown was born March 16, 1955, in New York. Raised Jewish, he became addicted to heroin and LSD as a teenage rock drummer before converting in 1971, at 16, during the Jesus Movement. He holds a PhD in Near Eastern Languages and Literatures from NYU (1985), making him one of the more academically credentialed figures in the charismatic world this site covers. He led the FIRE School of Ministry, founded AskDrBrown Ministries in 2005, and hosts The Line of Fire, a nationally syndicated radio and YouTube program.

Brown is a committed continuationist and one of the movement's most academically serious public defenders — his book Authentic Fire (2014) is a direct, extended rebuttal to John MacArthur's cessationist critique Strange Fire. That matters for how to read everything below: he isn't an outside critic of the charismatic movement, and he isn't a New Apostolic Reformation figure by C. Peter Wagner's original apostolic-network definition either. He's something more specific and more interesting — an insider who has repeatedly, publicly defended named NAR-adjacent leaders (Bill Johnson of Bethel, Ché Ahn, Mike Bickle of IHOP-KC among them, per NAR researcher Holly Pivec), while also being willing, at least once, to run an actual accountability process against one of the movement's own.

Brownsville: not the revival's leader, but its theological defender

Before any of that, Brown was already embedded in the movement's most physically extreme wing. The Brownsville Revival began June 18, 1995, at Brownsville Assembly of God in Pensacola, Florida — nightly services that ran for roughly five years, drawing an estimated two million visitors. It's worth being precise about who actually led it: the preaching was Steve Hill's, night after night; the host pastor was John Kilpatrick. Brown was not the revival's leader. His specific, documented role was founding and running the Brownsville Revival School of Ministry — first as dean, later as president — starting in January 1997, about a year and a half after the revival itself began.

What actually happened at those services is well documented: uncontrollable laughter, barking and roaring sounds described by one contemporaneous evangelical analysis as "a cross between a jungle and a farmyard," shaking, falling, temporary paralysis, and people reportedly acting "drunk" under the presumed influence of the Holy Spirit. Brownsville's leadership publicly insisted their revival was distinct from the similar, contemporaneous "Toronto Blessing" — but the manifestations reported at both were, by outside description, largely the same phenomenon. Brown didn't just tolerate this — he wrote entire books actively defending it: From Holy Laughter to Holy Fire: America on the Edge of Revival (1996) and Let No One Deceive You: Confronting the Critics of Revival (1997), the latter devoting dozens of pages specifically to answering hard questions about the manifestations.

He didn't leave Brownsville over any of that, though — the actual, well-documented reason is more mundane and more revealing about how he handles institutional authority generally. The Assemblies of God had made a multimillion-dollar loan for the school's campus and wanted accountability in return, including that Brown hold AG ministerial credentials. Brown refused, saying God had told him not to affiliate with the denomination. Kilpatrick gave him an ultimatum; the school's board fired him on December 17, 2000. Brown's own quote at the time: "You can fire me as being president, but you can't fire me as being father." He started a new, independent school — what's now FIRE School of Ministry — nearby almost immediately, taking much of the faculty and student body with him. The pattern is worth flagging early, since it recurs throughout this article: offered institutional accountability he doesn't control, Brown doesn't submit to it — he leaves and rebuilds on his own terms.

The Todd Bentley process — and a real nuance worth getting right

In August 2019, revivalist Stephen Powell publicly posted detailed misconduct allegations against Todd Bentley, including sexual misconduct involving both male and female interns. A group of charismatic leaders asked Brown to organize a fair, impartial review process — and it's worth being precise about what that meant in practice. By Brown's own explicit public statement, he did not serve on the panel that made the actual determination, and wasn't part of the decision-making itself; his role was to oversee the process, compile a 60-question list Bentley initially agreed to answer and then didn't follow through on, and coordinate an investigator gathering witness testimony over roughly three months. The five leaders who actually reached the verdict were Joseph Mattera, James Goll, Jane Hamon, Harry Jackson, and Don Finto.

On January 2, 2020, that panel declared Bentley "not qualified to serve in leadership or ministry today," citing "credible accusations of a steady pattern of ungodly and immoral behavior" from roughly 2004 to 2019 — adultery, sexting, vulgar language, substance abuse — and called it especially serious because some accusers had been under Bentley's spiritual authority. Brown issued his own follow-up statement the next day. This is a real, documented instance of the charismatic movement producing an internal accountability finding against one of its own biggest names — genuinely rarer than this site's other coverage might suggest is typical. It's also worth noting that cessationist critics mocked the process as insufficiently rigorous at the time, and that Bentley was platformed again by Rick Joyner in 2024 regardless of the panel's finding, as covered in that article — the panel's conclusion, however genuine, still had no actual enforcement power over what Bentley or his allies did next.

An intermediary in the Bickle scandal — a real tension

Brown was also involved as an intermediary in the 2023–2024 Mike Bickle sexual abuse scandal covered in this site's IHOP-KC article, publicly stating "both sides have made mistakes" while working to broker a mutually acceptable third-party investigator between IHOP-KC's leadership and an advocacy group representing accusers — talks that reportedly reached an impasse. Notice what that framing actually does: in a case about a founder accused of sexually abusing 17 people over decades, "both sides have made mistakes" places some of the fault on the side making the allegations. That's a real, on-record instance of Brown diffusing blame outward in a case involving someone he'd previously defended by name — Bickle is one of the leaders Holly Pivec documents him praising. A defender turned mediator, on a scandal involving someone he'd praised, is not the same thing as an independent investigator.

How he argues: the Justin Peters exchange

Worth understanding on its own terms, because it's a clean, on-record example of a rhetorical pattern that shows up again later in this article. On April 1, 2023 (publicly released March 2024), Brown and fellow continuationist Sam Storms sat across from cessationists Justin Peters and Jim Osman in a four-hour recorded roundtable on false teachers and prophecy, produced by American Gospel. Peters — a Southern Baptist evangelist with cerebral palsy who has spent years documenting Benny Hinn's crusade practices, and who's cited elsewhere on this site's Benny Hinn article — pressed the case that specific named charismatic figures should be classified as false teachers based on their documented conduct and doctrine.

Brown's response didn't engage that standard directly. Instead, he pushed back by asking whether Martin Luther — revered by cessationists like Peters — should also be called a false teacher, given Luther's own later-life writings calling for burning synagogues, writings later cited in Nazi propaganda. Brown's quote: "He promoted things that undermine the Gospel. Many Jewish people think that Christianity is miserable and despicable because of Martin Luther." Peters' reply stuck to the actual question in front of him: "Judging from the teachings that he gave concerning the Gospel, I can't on the basis of that... call him a false teacher." Notice what happened to the original question. Peters had raised specific, documented conduct by specific charismatic leaders; Brown's answer never returned to that — it relocated the entire debate to whether Luther's antisemitism disqualifies cessationists' own hero by their standard. That's a real rhetorical move, on video, not a strawman: raise a consistency challenge broad enough that it never has to circle back to the original, specific claim.

Then it was his own case

Brown was Sarah Monk's employer and the leader of the institution she worked for — she was a young secretary at FIRE School of Ministry, the school he founded and led. That authority imbalance is exactly why an independent investigator, not this site, concluded genuine consent was implausible. In November 2024, Brown stepped back from public ministry after misconduct allegations against him surfaced. Line of Fire's board commissioned Firefly Independent Sexual Abuse Investigations — the same firm that investigated Mike Bickle — led by investigator Jim Holler. Firefly's report, released in April 2025, is direct about what it found: Brown engaged in "sexually abusive misconduct" against Monk — hand-holding, kissing, touching, and lap-sitting — and, separately, an "emotional affair" with a married congregant that Brown himself acknowledged while denying physical contact.

The most damning finding isn't the misconduct itself — it's what the investigator documented about the decades afterward. Firefly described "10 different confrontations over 23 years" and characterized Brown's response across that entire span as "a calculated effort to evade accountability." That's not a single lapse followed by honest reckoning. That's a quarter-century pattern of being confronted, directly, repeatedly, by multiple people, and each time managing to talk, deflect, or wait his way out of it rather than actually addressing what he'd done — which is precisely what happened again in 2025, on a larger stage.

A separately convened "Elder Accountability Team" — Jonathan Bernis, Raleigh Washington, Leif Hetland, and Mark and Nicki Pfeifer, all people within Brown's own orbit — publicly rejected the independent investigator's "sexually abusive misconduct" finding, relabeled the same conduct "moral indiscretions," disputed the investigation's process, declared Brown "committed no sin for which he has not repented," and recommended he resume public ministry. Call that what it is: a euphemism swapped in for an investigator's actual finding, by a panel the subject of the investigation helped select. Days later, two of the investigation's own witnesses released a 134-page counter-report disputing the elder team's conclusions and asserting the pattern was more extensive, and the resistance to accountability longer-running, than the elder team's statement acknowledged. This site's most recent verified information on the matter dates to May 2025; if you know Brown's actual current ministry status, this is exactly the kind of open, still-developing case where that update matters.

Set side by side with the Bentley timeline above, the pattern is worth stating plainly: Brown built real credibility running an accountability process against someone else's twenty-year pattern of misconduct in 2020. When an outside investigator applied the same scrutiny to his own twenty-three-year pattern in 2025, the panel that actually had the final word wasn't outside anyone — it was hand-picked by his own side, and it spent its statement renaming what happened rather than reckoning with it. A person skilled at organizing accountability for other people's misconduct, and skilled at reframing and deflecting scrutiny of his own, are not in tension. Both are documented here, and both are the same set of skills pointed in different directions.

The politics

Brown backed Ted Cruz over Trump in the 2016 primary, later offering Trump conditional, transactional support — explicitly over judicial appointments, opposition to "radical Islam" and "radical LGBT activism," and support for Israel, while stating plainly that Trump "did not die for my sins." In October 2022, Brown co-authored, with Joseph Mattera, a public statement signed by 60-plus charismatic leaders explicitly repudiating violent Christian nationalism and "top-down takeover" dominionism — directly relevant territory for this site's own critique of the Seven Mountain Mandate. NAR researchers Holly Pivec and Douglas Geivett, along with Religion Dispatches, found the statement toothless: it named no specific leaders or teachings, using what they called strawmen with no facts attached. A statement against Christian-nationalist overreach that declines to name a single person or network practicing it isn't nothing, but it isn't accountability either.
