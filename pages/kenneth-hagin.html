Kenneth Hagin

The man widely credited as "father of the Word of Faith movement" — and the documented plagiarism behind his signature doctrine

This is a companion piece to Kenneth Copeland & "Enough Faith to Be Healed" — Copeland's doctrine didn't originate with him. Hagin is where it actually starts, and where the movement's foundational text-critical problem starts too. Where sources disagreed or a claim rested only on Hagin's own retrospective account, that's stated rather than smoothed into settled fact.

The founding story

Kenneth Erwin Hagin was born August 20, 1917, in McKinney, Texas, and died September 19, 2003, in Tulsa, at 86. By his own account — this is his own retrospective testimony, not an independently verified medical record — he was born with a deformed heart and what he described as an incurable blood disease, becoming paralyzed and bedridden at 15. He said he died and was revived multiple times in 1933, each time describing himself as dragged toward hell before being pulled back. A year or so later, on August 8, 1934, he said he was raised from his deathbed and healed after a revelation from Mark 11:23–24 — "whatsoever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them." That verse, read as a literal formula rather than a general exhortation to prayer, becomes the entire theological engine of everything that follows: Hagin's, and later Copeland's, whole doctrine of faith as a mechanism you operate rather than a trust you hold.

He founded a non-denominational church in 1936, became a licensed Assemblies of God minister in 1937, and pastored Assemblies of God churches until 1949, when he left to begin independent, itinerant ministry — this site could not confirm whether that departure involved a formal surrender of his AG credentials or was simply a voluntary exit. In 1963 he formed the Kenneth E. Hagin Evangelistic Association; the ministry relocated to Tulsa in 1966 under the "Rhema" name; and in 1974, RHEMA Bible Training College opened on a former farm in Broken Arrow, Oklahoma, graduating its first class of 58 the following spring.

How big RHEMA actually got

The numbers vary depending on the year and what's being counted, and this site isn't going to average them into one clean figure that sounds more precise than the sourcing allows: one academic history cites roughly 16,000 RHEMA alumni from nearly 30 countries by the mid-1990s; RHEMA's own more recent promotional material claims 123,000 graduates since 1974. What's not in dispute is the reach: Rhema Bible Church grew to around 8,000 members, Hagin's Faith Library Publications distributed more than 65 million books — nearly all authored by Hagin himself — and his Word of Faith magazine reached a quarter million subscribers. RHEMA is widely credited, including by Kenneth Copeland himself, as the training ground behind a significant share of the modern prosperity-gospel preaching landscape, though it's worth being precise about who actually trained where: Copeland attended Hagin's traveling seminars starting in 1967, seven years before RHEMA's college existed, so he's a protégé of Hagin personally, not a RHEMA graduate. Several other prosperity preachers commonly grouped into "Hagin's circle" in secondary coverage — Creflo Dollar, Jerry Savelle, Jesse Duplantis, Charles Capps — don't have their RHEMA attendance confirmed in either their own public biographies or independent sourcing this site could verify; treat that grouping as "doctrinally downstream of Hagin," which is well established, not "personally trained at RHEMA," which mostly isn't.

The plagiarism, documented side by side

This is the part of Hagin's legacy that isn't seriously disputed by religious historians, whatever RHEMA-aligned defenders say about it. In 1984, ORU graduate student Dale H. Simmons — who went on to become a credentialed academic, later publishing E.W. Kenyon and the Postbellum Pursuit of Christian Character through Scarecrow Press — documented that Hagin's 1967 booklet The Authority of the Believer closely paralleled a 1932 booklet with the identical title by missionary John A. MacMillan, alleging word-for-word copying without attribution.

D.R. McConnell's 1988 book A Different Gospel — the standard critical history of the movement — goes further, stating plainly: "Hagin has, indeed, copied word-for-word without documentation from Kenyon's writings," citing examples across no fewer than eight of E.W. Kenyon's books, and noting the examples given are representative, not exhaustive. The Christian Research Institute lays out one specific, checkable comparison: Kenyon's 1964 book The Father and His Family (p. 100) reads, "Every man who has been 'born again' is an Incarnation... The believer is as much an Incarnation as was Jesus." Hagin's own Word of Faith magazine, December 1980 (p. 14), runs the nearly identical line, without attribution. Hagin's most-quoted defense, from a 1986 letter responding to the charge, was that the verbatim overlap was itself evidence of shared divine inspiration — "it is the same Spirit that is leading and directing." Sympathetic defenders have separately argued his books were sermon transcriptions rather than written compositions, that he may have absorbed Kenyon's phrasing secondhand through other preachers rather than by reading Kenyon directly, or that he had a "photographic memory" that reproduced text without conscious awareness of its source. This site could not find any formal statement, correction, or acknowledgment from RHEMA or Kenneth Hagin Ministries as an institution — only Hagin's own personal letter.

Kate Bowler's Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel (Oxford University Press, 2013) — the standard academic history of the movement, not a polemical source — treats Hagin's dependence on Kenyon as settled background rather than a live dispute, identifying RHEMA as a major launching pad for the modern prosperity movement. Pushback on the plagiarism finding comes mainly from movement-sympathetic writers, not from historians of American religion.

"We are Christ": the doctrine itself

Hagin claimed roughly eight personal visitations from Jesus over his ministry, documented in his 1984 book I Believe in Visions — including a 1950 Phoenix vision in which Jesus reportedly gave him formula-like "keys" to answered prayer ("According to your action you receive or you are kept from receiving"), a 1958 vision in which Jesus introduced him to his own personal angel, and a January 2002 account, published in Word of Faith magazine, of an ascension into heaven itself.

The doctrine that follows from this — and that Copeland later inherited and built a career on — is that believers aren't just forgiven, they're a different category of being. From Hagin's 1989 book Zoe: The God-Kind of Life: "Man was created on terms of equality with God, and he could stand in God's presence without any consciousness of inferiority.... God has made us as much like Himself as possible.... He made us the same class of being that He is Himself.... [T]he believer is called Christ.... That's who we are; we're Christ!" And from The Incarnation, p. 12 — this specific line is the same one documented above as lifted from Kenyon, worth sitting with for a moment: the passage that defines what a born-again Christian actually is in Hagin's system is itself not original to him. "Every man who has been born again is an incarnation and Christianity is a miracle. The believer is as much an incarnation as was Jesus of Nazareth."

The most theologically serious charge against Hagin, though, isn't the "little gods" language — it's what's usually shortened to "JDS," Jesus Died Spiritually. Hagin taught that Christ's death on the cross wasn't the completed atonement; that his spirit actually went to hell, where he was tormented by Satan and demons for three days, took on a satanic or sinful nature, and had to be "born again" himself in hell before his resurrection. Direct quote: "He [Jesus] tasted spiritual death for every man. And His spirit and inner men went to hell in my place." A 2007 University of Edinburgh doctoral thesis, the most rigorous academic treatment of this specific doctrine, traces its lineage precisely: "JDS teaching originated with Kenyon, was introduced to the Word-faith movement by Hagin, and continues to be offered by Copeland" — and concludes the doctrine is flatly incompatible with the historic Christian understanding of the Trinity, the incarnation, and substitutionary atonement. This is the same doctrinal chain covered in the Copeland article, traced back to where it actually starts.

A prediction that didn't happen, and a death that raises the same question Copeland's does

In 1997, Hagin delivered a prophecy at a "Holy Ghost Meeting" predicting a major spiritual outpouring beginning that October in St. Louis, which would "spread all over the nation" through 1998, 1999, and 2000. Its language is vague enough to resist a clean pass/fail verdict — which is itself the pattern this site's main article identifies as characteristic of NAR-adjacent prophecy generally — but no source documents the specific October 1997 St. Louis outpouring occurring at anything like the scale predicted.

Hagin's own death raises the same question his protégé Copeland's pacemaker does, though the record here is thinner. He went to bed feeling well on Saturday, September 13, 2003; the next morning, at breakfast, he sighed and his head fell to his chest in front of his wife. He was admitted to a cardiac intensive care unit and died six days later. This site could not find a publicly stated official cause of death, nor any account — from Hagin himself or people close to him — grappling directly with what a sudden fatal cardiac event means for a ministry built entirely on the premise that sufficient faith guarantees healing. The absence of that reckoning is itself worth noting: the doctrine doesn't have a mechanism for examining its own failure, in Hagin's case any more than in Copeland's.

What's left

RHEMA Bible Training College continues operating in Broken Arrow under Hagin's son, Kenneth Hagin Jr., who has led both the church and the ministry since his father's death. The doctrinal lineage traced above — Kenyon to Hagin to Copeland, and from Copeland outward to Benny Hinn, Creflo Dollar, and the wider Word of Faith/prosperity landscape this site has covered — is, per Kate Bowler's academic history, the actual backbone of the modern American prosperity gospel. Hagin is the least publicly visible of the figures in that chain today, precisely because he did the foundational work three decades before it became a televised media empire in other people's hands.
