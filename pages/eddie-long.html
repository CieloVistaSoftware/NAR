Bishop Eddie Long & New Birth

A self-styled "king," four accusers, and a confidential settlement

Biographical, not a theological critique of Baptist megachurch structure generally. Where a specific figure (like the settlement amount) was never officially disclosed, that's stated rather than repeated as fact.

From 300 members to 25,000

Eddie Long became pastor of New Birth Missionary Baptist Church, in Lithonia, Georgia, in 1987, when it had roughly 300 members. He built it into one of the largest Black megachurches in the country, peaking at around 25,000 members. In 1994 he was consecrated as the third presiding bishop of the Full Gospel Baptist Church Fellowship — a real, if young and doctrinally loose, breakaway network formed to let Baptist megachurch leaders take on charismatic/episcopal titles. New Birth ended that formal affiliation in 2008; no source documents what accountability structure, if any, governed Long's continued use of the "bishop" title for the remaining decade of his ministry.

His compensation drew scrutiny well before the scandal that ended his reputation. Between 1997 and 2000 he received more than $3 million from his ministry nonprofit, including $494,000 in salary in 2000 alone, alongside a $1.4 million estate, a $350,000 Bentley, and a private jet — disclosures that helped prompt a 2011 U.S. Senate Finance Committee investigation into lavish spending across six televangelist ministries. That investigation closed with no formal findings against him.

In January 2012, Long invited a self-styled "Rabbi" Ralph Messer — whose own claim to be Jewish was publicly disputed — to a New Birth service, where Messer wrapped him in a Torah scroll and ceremonially crowned him "king." Jewish organizations, including the Anti-Defamation League, objected; Long publicly apologized, saying he "sincerely denounce[d] any action that depicts me as a King, for I am merely just a servant of the Lord." No source documents a formal tie between Long and New Apostolic Reformation apostolic networks specifically — his real, well-documented circle ran through Word of Faith/prosperity-gospel figures (T.D. Jakes, Paula White, Kenneth Copeland) via shared conference circuits — but the Torah "king" episode is itself a striking, independently confirmed instance of exactly the kind of self-conferred, unaccountable spiritual authority this site's critique is about.

The lawsuits

In September 2010, four young men who had passed through New Birth's "Longfellows" youth ministry — Jamal Parris, Anthony Flagg, Maurice Robinson, and Spencer LeGrande — filed civil lawsuits alleging Long used his position as their spiritual mentor to coerce them into sexual relationships that began when they were teenagers. Their complaints described being put on the church payroll, given cars and gifts, taken on lavish trips including overseas travel and private-jet flights, and told the relationships were spiritually sanctioned. A fifth accuser, Centino Kemp, came forward during mediation.

This proceeded as civil litigation rather than a criminal case because the young men were past Georgia's age of consent (16) when the alleged relationships occurred — not, as sometimes assumed, because of a statute-of-limitations bar.

The settlement, and what happened after

All five plaintiffs settled with Long in May 2011. The terms were confidential, and the settlement amount was never officially disclosed — reported estimates in the press have ranged anywhere from roughly $1.5 million to $15 million total, none of them confirmed, and this site isn't going to repeat a specific figure as fact when no reliable source actually has one. Long denied the underlying allegations throughout and never admitted wrongdoing. That October, his attorneys sought to claw back settlement funds, alleging some plaintiffs had violated the confidentiality terms by discussing the case publicly. He remained New Birth's senior pastor for the rest of his life.

Death

In September 2016, after visibly dramatic weight loss, Long publicly acknowledged an unnamed "health challenge" and described following a raw-vegetable "eating for life" diet as part of what he called "a holistic approach to good health" — stating at the time that the diet was "unrelated" to whatever the underlying issue was, and that he was "not on any medications." He died January 15, 2017, at 63; New Birth's statement gave the cause as "an aggressive form of cancer," without naming the type or disclosing any treatment history. No source confirms that he was ever offered, or refused, conventional cancer treatment in favor of the diet — the two facts (the raw-food regimen, and the later cancer death) are both real and documented, but no reporting connects them into the specific "chose diet over chemotherapy" narrative some readers may expect. That distinction matters, and this site isn't going to blur it just because the pattern would fit neatly into its broader critique of faith-over-medicine teaching.
