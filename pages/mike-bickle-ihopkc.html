Mike Bickle & IHOP-KC

A founding architect of NAR's apostolic governance, and what an independent investigation found

This is the single most directly NAR-relevant scandal on this site — not adjacent to the movement, but built by one of the people who helped structure it. The allegations here involve serious, credibly documented sexual abuse; this article sticks to what independent investigation has actually found; sourcing throughout.

Not just a prayer house — a founding architect of NAR's apostolic structure

Mike Bickle founded the International House of Prayer Kansas City (IHOP-KC) on May 7, 1999, built around a "harp and bowl" worship model and a Global Prayer Room that has run live, 24 hours a day, since September of that year. Before that, he led the Kansas City Fellowship through the 1980s and '90s and was central to the "Kansas City Prophets" circle before separating from the Vineyard movement.

Bickle's connection to the New Apostolic Reformation isn't incidental — he was a charter member of the Apostolic Council of Prophetic Elders (ACPE), formed June 30, 1999 under C. Peter Wagner's leadership specifically as a governing body meant to supersede traditional denominational accountability, alongside fellow charter members Cindy Jacobs, Dutch Sheets, Chuck Pierce, Jim Goll, Bill Hamon, and the Wagners themselves — several of the same names that recur throughout this site's main article and People & Organizations page. Historian Matthew Taylor, who studies the charismatic movement, describes IHOP-KC as one of the key hubs pushing "the full release of the apostolic" through the 1990s and 2000s — embedding the theology this site critiques across a wide network of ministries.

The allegations

On October 28, 2023, a group of former IHOP-KC leaders publicly released a statement accusing Bickle of a pattern of clergy sexual abuse spanning decades. Deborah Perkins was the first survivor to be publicly identified, saying Bickle groomed and sexually abused her from 1996 to 1999, beginning when she was 19 and he was 42. Bickle went on sabbatical the next day pending an internal investigation.

IHOP-KC's first internal review, that November, reportedly cast doubt on the allegations rather than substantiating them — a response later criticized as inadequate. In early December 2023, Bickle issued a statement: "I sadly admit that 20+ years ago, I sinned by engaging in inappropriate behavior," while explicitly denying "more intense sexual activities." Within weeks, on December 22–23, 2023, IHOP-KC's Executive Committee announced a permanent separation from Bickle, saying "new information" now confirmed "a level of inappropriate behavior," and hired an outside law firm to investigate further. In February 2024, the Kansas City Star reported a further account: a woman alleging Bickle groomed and abused her beginning in the 1980s, when she was 14 and he was reportedly her family's babysitter; a second woman said abuse of her began at 15.

Two investigations, and what they found

IHOP-KC commissioned law firm Lathrop GPM to investigate; its report concluded Bickle "more likely than not" engaged in sexual misconduct, though some earlier accusers declined to cooperate, questioning whether an internally-commissioned probe could really be independent.

A separate, more thorough investigation followed after 16 former IHOP-KC leaders requested outside involvement: Tikkun Global, a Messianic Jewish organization with no affiliation to IHOP-KC, funded and commissioned Firefly Independent Sexual Abuse Investigations, which interviewed 210 people and reviewed more than 6,000 documents. Firefly's report, released February 3, 2025, concluded Bickle perpetrated "sexually abusive misconduct, including sexual abuse, rape, clergy abuse, and spiritual abuse" against 17 survivors, dating back "to our knowledge" to the mid-1970s. The report also found IHOP-KC leadership showed "deliberate indifference," and used internal "Matthew 18" conflict-resolution meetings — a biblical model meant for peer accountability — to conceal, minimize, and silence victims rather than investigate leadership, and actively discouraged survivors from going to police. IHOP-KC has not formally endorsed the Firefly report's findings.

In September 2025, a Tikkun-affiliated Pastoral Recommendation Team issued a revised report recommending Bickle be "permanently disqualified from holding any such high and honorable office anywhere in the Body of Christ in his lifetime," and that three other named leaders — Stuart Greaves, David Sliker, and Lenny La Guardia — step away from public ministry for two years over their handling of the allegations. IHOP-KC's executive director responded that the organization disagreed with "the full scope of the Firefly report" but said the recommendations were "taken into consideration."

Outcome

No criminal charges have been filed against Bickle as of the most recent reporting; one pastor has publicly called on the Missouri Attorney General to pursue charges, but that's an advocacy call, not a prosecution. At least one civil lawsuit has been filed against IHOP-KC and an affiliated fellowship over a separate incident involving a minor during the same period. In March 2025, survivors testified before Missouri lawmakers in support of legislation to remove the statute of limitations for civil child-sex-abuse suits and to ban NDAs in related settlements, citing this case directly.

IHOP-KC did not close. Its "Forerunner Church" congregation held a final service, but the core prayer-room ministry continues under executive director Joseph Taylor and his wife Heba Taylor, who in a December 2025 fundraising email announced plans to relaunch a "healing and prophecy room" and a "school of worship" in 2026 — explicitly despite the Pastoral Recommendation Team's March 2025 call for leadership changes. The livestream reportedly still reaches roughly 5,000 viewers a month.

Charismatic apologist Michael Brown — who, notably, had previously defended Bickle by name — got involved as an intermediary between IHOP-KC's leadership and accusers' advocates, publicly saying "both sides have made mistakes" while trying to broker a mutually acceptable independent investigator; those talks reportedly reached an impasse.

What this actually says about NAR's structure

This isn't just this site's own argument — it's what people who covered the case directly concluded. Julie Roys, whose outlet The Roys Report has published the most extensive reporting on this story, quotes Matthew Taylor describing the apostolic/prophetic culture as having "evolved … to make clergy sexual abuse not only likely, but almost inevitable," calling it a "spiritual oligarchy" in which ordinary members have no mechanism to remove a leader they never elected. A Bethel School of Supernatural Ministry alumnus quoted in the same piece puts it more sharply: reform can't fix this, because "the problems are rooted in the idea of prophecy and what it even means to be an apostle" — a self-styled prophet can retain authority even after a failed prophecy or credible abuse allegation, because there's no external body with the power to revoke the office in the first place. That's the exact structural failure this site's main article argues NAR's apostolic model produces — and here, it's documented by an independent 68-page investigation, not just critics.
