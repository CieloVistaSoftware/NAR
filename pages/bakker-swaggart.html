Jim Bakker & Jimmy Swaggart

The two scandals that taught American televangelism it could survive anything

Biographical and factual — the two foundational scandals of the modern televangelism era, six months apart in 1987–88. Where sources disagree on an exact figure or date, that's noted rather than picked arbitrarily.

Jim Bakker: Heritage USA, a hush-money payment, and 24 felony counts

Jim Bakker founded PTL ("Praise the Lord," later "People That Love") in 1974; by the mid-1980s The PTL Club reached an estimated 13 million households, the highest-rated religious program in the country. He and Tammy Faye built Heritage USA, a Christian theme park and residential complex in Fort Mill, South Carolina, that grew into one of the most-visited theme parks in America. In his early ministry Bakker preached prosperity theology outright — his 1980 book stated "God wants you to be happy, God wants you to be rich, God wants you to prosper" — before publicly renouncing that teaching in a 1996 book, I Was Wrong.

In 1987, journalist Charles Shepard of the Charlotte Observer exposed that PTL had paid $279,000 in ministry funds to Jessica Hahn, a former church secretary, connected to a 1980 sexual encounter with Bakker that she said was assault and he said was consensual. Bakker resigned March 19, 1987. He asked Jerry Falwell Sr. to take over temporarily; Falwell's team found PTL $65 million in debt and losing $2 million a month, and by October had relinquished control, publicly calling the operation a "scab and cancer on the face of Christianity." PTL filed for bankruptcy that June.

The bankruptcy exposed the real fraud: Bakker had sold "lifetime partnerships" at $1,000 each, promising annual free lodging at Heritage USA — capping one tier publicly at 25,000 partnerships while actually selling far more (nearly 160,000 partnerships cumulatively by one count), collecting well over $100 million for accommodations that could never exist for everyone who paid. He was indicted in December 1988 and convicted in October 1989 on all 24 counts: 8 of mail fraud, 15 of wire fraud, and 1 of conspiracy. The trial judge sentenced him to 45 years — a sentence the Fourth Circuit vacated in 1991, finding the judge had shown bias in remarks about "money-grubbing preachers." Resentenced to 8 years, Bakker was released in 1994 after serving roughly four and a half. Tammy Faye died of cancer in 2007.

Bakker returned to television in 2003 with an apocalyptic, survivalist-supply-focused program based in Missouri. In March 2020, the state's attorney general sued him for marketing a product called Silver Solution as a COVID-19 cure; he settled in June 2021, paying $156,000 and agreeing not to market the product as treating, preventing, or curing any disease, without admitting wrongdoing.

Jimmy Swaggart: exposed by a rival, defiant the second time

Jimmy Swaggart — a double first cousin of Jerry Lee Lewis, and a cousin of Mickey Gilley, all three raised together in Ferriday, Louisiana — built one of the largest television ministries in the country from a 1971 start, airing on more than 250 stations by 1983. He was, notably, also one of the earliest and most persistent critics of the Word of Faith movement itself, publishing a piece attacking what he called "hyper-faith" theology as early as 1982.

In 1986, Swaggart had accused fellow Assemblies of God minister Marvin Gorman of adultery, getting Gorman defrocked. Gorman retaliated by having Swaggart surveilled; his son and son-in-law photographed Swaggart with a prostitute, Debra Murphree, outside a Louisiana motel, and Gorman reported it to denominational leadership in February 1988. Murphree later told reporters they hadn't had intercourse, but that Swaggart paid her over roughly twenty encounters to pose nude and perform other acts.

On February 21, 1988, Swaggart delivered a televised confession before his own congregation: "I have sinned against You, my Lord, and I would ask that Your precious blood would wash and cleanse every stain." The Assemblies of God initially suspended him three months with a two-year rehabilitation requirement; when he tried to return to the pulpit early instead of completing it, the national denomination formally revoked his ministerial credentials that April. He broke from the Assemblies of God entirely and continued independently.

In October 1991, California Highway Patrol stopped Swaggart for driving on the wrong side of the road; a prostitute, Rosemary Garcia, was in the car. This time there was no tearful confession. He told his congregation flatly: "The Lord told me it's flat none of your business." He stepped back from the pulpit briefly but underwent nothing like the 1988 rehabilitation process. Jimmy Swaggart Ministries continued as an independent operation, eventually building SonLife Broadcasting Network; he died in July 2025 at 90.

Did this change anything?

The Evangelical Council for Financial Accountability, often cited as evangelicalism's financial self-policing body, actually predates both scandals by nearly a decade — it was founded in 1979 after a U.S. senator warned ministries to self-regulate or face government intervention. PTL was itself an ECFA member from 1981 through late 1986, reportedly warned about irregularities and told not to use the ECFA seal — and used it anyway, without consequence, until after the scandal became public. That's cited by watchdog groups today as a case study in exactly how little a voluntary accountability seal is actually worth when the organization wearing it doesn't want to comply.
