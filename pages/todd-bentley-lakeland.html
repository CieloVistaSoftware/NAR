Todd Bentley & the Lakeland Revival

C. Peter Wagner personally commissioned him. He kept coming back anyway.

Biographical and factual. Two details sometimes repeated about this case turned out not to check out under sourcing — flagged explicitly below rather than repeated.

The revivalist

Todd Bentley, born in British Columbia in 1976, was convicted at 15 of sexually assaulting a younger boy, and struggled with drug and alcohol addiction as a teenager before a conversion at 18. By 1998 he led Fresh Fire Ministries. He preached and prayed for healing with visibly physical methods — kicking, hitting, or knocking people over, which he described as Holy Spirit-led — with specific documented incidents including a man who lost a tooth after being knocked down and an elderly woman he intentionally kicked in the face. In 2001 he claimed an encounter with a female-presenting angel he called "Emma," who he said sprinkled "gold dust" over a congregation — a claim the Assemblies of God's general superintendent publicly rejected as extra-biblical.

The revival, and an actual apostolic commissioning ceremony

In April 2008, Bentley was invited to Ignited Church in Lakeland, Florida for what was planned as a one-week revival. It ran more than four months. At its peak it drew up to 10,000 people nightly, with more than 400,000 total visitors from over 100 countries reported by the end of June, livestreamed and broadcast nightly on GOD TV. Leaders claimed at least 25 resurrections from the dead occurring off-site — no body was ever produced at a meeting — and when the magazine WORLD later followed up on 13 specific named healing claims Fresh Fire provided as evidence, it found one person had returned to cancer treatment, another still had the condition claimed healed, and two had died of exactly the conditions they were said to be cured of.

On June 23, 2008, C. Peter Wagner — head of what became the International Coalition of Apostolic Leaders, and the figure who coined "New Apostolic Reformation" — led a public ceremony in Lakeland with at least sixteen other charismatic leaders present, including Ché Ahn, Bill Johnson, John Arnott, and Rick Joyner laying hands on Bentley. Wagner asked the others whether they recognized "the grace of God given to Todd Bentley," and, after they affirmed it, declared: "This commissioning represents a powerful spiritual transaction taking place in the invisible world," decreeing that Bentley's "power will increase, your authority will increase, your favor will increase, your influence will increase, your revelation will increase." Contemporaneous reporting describes this specifically as commissioning Bentley as an evangelist, not formally ordaining him "apostle" — but it's exactly the kind of unaccountable, ceremony-conferred spiritual authority this site's main article argues is NAR's central structural flaw, and Wagner himself is the one who proposed holding it.

The collapse

On August 12, 2008, Bentley announced he and his wife Shonnah — with whom he had three children — were separating. Three days later, Fresh Fire's board disclosed he had "entered into an unhealthy relationship on an emotional level with a female member of his staff." The staff member was later identified as Jessa Hasbrook, who had worked as a nanny for the Bentley family; two charismatic leaders, including Wagner himself, later said publicly they believed the relationship had in fact been sexual, not merely emotional as first disclosed. Bentley resigned from Fresh Fire's board; the revival sputtered on with visiting speakers and ended by October.

Restoration, and how fast it moved

Rick Joyner of MorningStar Ministries — describing himself as Bentley's "spiritual father" — led a restoration effort with Jack Deere and Bill Johnson. Bentley relocated to MorningStar's home base in Fort Mill, South Carolina. He married Jessa in March 2009, while his divorce from Shonnah was still being finalized. By 2010, Joyner had already released him into "limited ministry" — a pace of restoration that was, by multiple accounts, "widely condemned even by those in the same charismatic circles." In 2012 the UK government issued an exclusion order barring Bentley from entering the country, citing his "unorthodox and often violent healing methods."

Two corrections worth stating plainly: Bentley did not face a documented 2011 domestic-violence conviction or restraining order — no such record could be found despite a specific search, and that detail appears to be a misremembering. And his remarriage to Jessa was in 2009, not 2012 — the UK exclusion order is the actual 2012 event.

Then it happened again

In 2019, former protégé Stephen Powell publicly accused Bentley of a pattern of sexual harassment and soliciting nude photos from interns and staff, alleging his wife Jessa helped cover for it. Charismatic apologist Michael Brown organized and oversaw an investigative process — five other leaders made the actual determination — that interviewed dozens of people about conduct dating from 2004 to 2019. In January 2020 the panel declared Bentley "disqualified from ministry," citing "credible accusations of adultery, sexting (including the exchanging of nude pictures or videos), vulgar language, and substance abuse" and finding "no signs of true, lasting repentance." Bentley admitted to "inappropriate texting," disputed the rest, and called the process a "witch trial."

None of that stopped him. As of late 2022 reporting he was running revival events under the name Revival Harvest Ministries, with engagements scheduled into 2025. In December 2024, Rick Joyner invited him back to speak at MorningStar's Vision Conference — introducing him with "we love this guy, haven't seen enough of him lately, he kind of disappeared" — drawing renewed public criticism that platforming him again disregarded a disqualification that was never actually lifted.

Why this one matters more than a personal scandal

A religion-journalism retrospective on Bentley's case makes the point directly: the charismatic movement's lack of formal denominational oversight, combined with a theology where a leader's calling is treated as directly divine and therefore not subject to institutional scrutiny, is why someone can survive scandal after scandal — the structure prefers narrative redemption over demonstrated accountability. A 2025 analysis of the disqualification panel's actual authority put it plainly: "the panel had no real authority to remove Bentley from ministry altogether — all they could do was advise others... that they believed Bentley to be unfit for leadership." An advisory opinion with no enforcement mechanism, against a man Wagner himself once "commissioned" in a public ceremony — that's the whole structural argument of this site's main article, playing out in one person's career twice.
