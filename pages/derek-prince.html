Derek Prince

Cambridge scholar, wartime medic, and the theologian whose teaching launched the Shepherding movement

This article is biographical, not a theological critique — that's covered in The New Apostolic Reformation and referenced throughout the Introduction. Every factual claim below is sourced; where sources disagreed or a detail couldn't be confirmed, that's noted rather than smoothed over.

From Cambridge to the Army

Derek Prince was born August 14, 1915, in Bangalore, British India, into a British military family. He won a scholarship to Eton College, then went on to King's College, Cambridge, where he held a Fellowship in Ancient and Modern Philosophy — a serious academic credential, not an honorary one. By his own later account, his Cambridge years left him an atheist: "I knew a lot of long words and phrases, and had tried a lot of different things. But, looking back, I would have to admit I was confused and frustrated."

In 1940 he enlisted in the Royal Army Medical Corps as a non-combatant — a deliberate choice, reflecting his own convictions at the time. On July 31, 1941, while stationed at a training barracks in Scarborough, Yorkshire, he described a sudden, vivid religious conversion experience: "I heard the voice of Jesus, speaking very very clearly through the scriptures, the Bible." He spent the next three years serving as an army medic in the deserts of North Africa — Egypt, Libya, and Sudan.

Jerusalem, not Africa: where he actually met Lydia

It's a common enough conflation that it's worth stating precisely: Prince's military service took him to North Africa, but the orphanage that changed the rest of his life was not there. He was discharged from the army while stationed in Jerusalem after the war ended, and it was in that region — sources describe the home as near Jerusalem, in Ramallah — that he met Lydia Christensen, a Danish missionary running a children's home caring for eight adopted girls, six of them Jewish. He married her in 1946 and became father to all eight; the couple later adopted a ninth daughter together.

After Israel's founding in 1948 and the conflict that followed, the family relocated to London, where Prince preached at Speaker's Corner in Hyde Park. In 1957 — and this is the real Africa connection in his story, over a decade after his wartime service there — the family moved to Kenya as missionaries, where Prince later described witnessing what he took to be miraculous healings, including a child he believed was raised from death through prayer.

Minneapolis, "Fort Lauderdale," and the Shepherding movement

By 1962 the family had moved to Minneapolis, where Prince became a Bible teacher and launched the daily radio program "Today with Derek Prince," eventually reaching a large network of stations. It was from this base, in the early-to-mid 1970s, that Prince became one of the "Fort Lauderdale Five" — alongside Don Basham and Charles Simpson, plus Bob Mumford and Ern Baxter — teaching a doctrine of direct, personal submission to an assigned spiritual "shepherd." See the Glossary for how that teaching connects to the New Apostolic Reformation this site critiques.

Prince was the first of the group to break away, in 1983, doing so privately — a letter to friends and colleagues rather than a public statement. He explained his reasoning six years later, on the same November 1989 evening Bob Mumford delivered his own public repentance at a conference in Ridgecrest, North Carolina: not a statement of repentance, in Prince's own framing, but of explanation. "I allowed loyalty to my fellow ministers to supersede my loyalty to God," he said. The fuller account of that evening, including the actual text of Mumford's statement, is in the Don Basham & Charles Simpson article.

Later ministry and death

Prince's second wife, Lydia, died; in 1978 he married Ruth Baker, an American single mother of three adopted children, whom he met in Jerusalem. His teaching ministry — recorded sermons, books, and Derek Prince Ministries, which continues today — outlasted the Shepherding controversy by two decades. He died September 24, 2003, in Jerusalem, at age 88, of natural causes.
