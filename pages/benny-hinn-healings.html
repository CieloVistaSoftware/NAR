Benny Hinn: Has He Ever Healed Anyone?

Five separate investigative teams, three countries, twenty-five years — and not one verified case

This site's main article already covers Benny Hinn's biography, his doctrinal record, and his finances. This is a dedicated, much deeper look at one specific claim: that his ministry has never produced a single independently verified healing, despite four decades of crusades and hundreds of millions of viewers. That's not this site's own assertion — it's what happened every time someone with real investigative resources went and checked. Where sourcing was thin or secondary, that's flagged rather than presented as settled.

What a full year of follow-up found: nothing

In 2001, HBO's documentary series America Undercover sent director Antony Thomas to follow seven specific people who had been presented as healed at Hinn (and, separately, Reinhard Bonnke) crusades, tracking them for roughly a year to see whether the healings actually held up. Both ministries gave the crew access. The finding, stated on CNN the day the documentary aired: they found no case where a person had actually been healed. Thomas put it more sharply to the New York Times: "If I had seen miracles, I would have been happy to trumpet it... but in retrospect, I think they do more damage to Christianity than the most committed atheist."

Screening out the cases that would actually prove something

CBC's investigative program the fifth estate, reported by Bob McKeown, aired two separate Hinn investigations, in late 2004 ("Do You Believe in Miracles?") and February 2008 ("Amazing Grace"). Using hidden cameras among crusade attendees, the 2004 investigation documented staff intercepting people with visibly, obviously apparent disabilities — wheelchair users, amputees — before they could reach the stage or healing line, redirecting them back to their seats. One of those intercepted, on camera, was Justin Peters, a Southern Baptist evangelist with cerebral palsy who walks with arm crutches and had written his graduate thesis on Hinn's ministry specifically to document it. His account: "People who look like me are never allowed on stage. It's always somebody who has some disability or disease that cannot be readily seen." Years later, summarizing eighteen crusades' worth of firsthand observation and follow-up: "People get up out of wheelchairs all the time at Benny Hinn crusades. I've been to 18 of them and have seen it hundreds of times. I've also followed up with many of them later. Do you know how many were truly healed? Zero. None."

That's the mechanism, stated plainly by someone who watched it happen repeatedly: a healing line that filters, before anyone reaches a camera, for cases where "getting up and walking" can't be independently checked against anything — and screens out the cases where it could be.

What happened when other investigators checked

This isn't one outlet's finding. Over roughly a decade and a half, independent teams using different methods reached the same result.

Inside Edition (1993) ran medical follow-up on specific cases: a brain-cancer patient Hinn had declared healed on stage still had the tumor on a subsequent scan; a woman "cured" of deafness turned out, according to her own doctor, to have never actually been deaf. Hinn told the crew and Trinity Foundation president Ole Anthony he'd obtain medical verification before broadcasting future testimonials, and separately promised to trade his Mercedes for a Honda — he did neither, and was still airing unverified testimonials months later. In a demonstration of how little vetting actually happened, an Inside Edition staffer fabricated a cerebral palsy healing, and Hinn's own program broadcast it as genuine.

60 Minutes Australia (1998) opened its segment by calling Hinn "a dangerous fake," tracked down people previously presented as healed, and found none had actually been cured — including one woman who had stopped taking prescribed medication on the strength of the claim. Hinn's own response on the record: neither he nor his staff would recommend anyone stop medication or doctor visits — a disclaimer that doesn't undo what his own crusade had already told her.

Dateline NBC (2002) requested medical proof of healings and was refused. Its most serious documented case — reported consistently across secondary accounts, though this site could not independently locate the original NBC transcript, so treat it as corroborated rather than primary-verified — involved a mother and son with terminal cancer brought on stage in front of roughly 14,000 people in Worcester, Massachusetts; Hinn embraced them and said the cancer would leave the household. The son died of cancer weeks later. The mother died of cancer about a month after Dateline's own follow-up interview with her. She said she never received a follow-up call from Hinn's organization.

The Los Angeles Times (2003), in William Lobdell's investigation "The Price of Healing," reported Hinn's ministry took in $89 million in donations in 2002 alone — and that of the "thousands" of healings the ministry has claimed, the single case its own defenders point to with any independent medical documentation is one doctor's letter, for one patient (Raymond Scott, a Bakersfield man whose physician reported an unexpected recovery from advanced colon cancer after a Hinn crusade) — not a controlled or blinded assessment, and by the reporting's own framing, the exception being used to represent the whole.

James Randi's James Randi Educational Foundation (2006) documented a Copenhagen crusade using footage from a Danish TV producer, finding the same pattern Randi had documented in other faith healers for decades: pre-screening participants to exclude visible disabilities, blaming "failed" healings on the recipient's insufficient faith, and adrenaline-driven crowd excitement being mistaken for physical healing. Hinn's team ordered cameras shut off during parts of the event. One case documented at that crusade: a 9-year-old girl in a wheelchair with spinal damage was not healed; her father, asked about it afterward, said only, "Today, it may be Kisser's turn... We don't know if it is, but we will keep on, until it is."

Named people, and what actually happened to them

  • William Vandenkolk — declared healed of blindness on stage at age 9, at a 2001 Las Vegas crusade; Hinn told him his vision would be "20/20 if not better." ABC's Nightline found him still legally blind years later. His own words: "I got caught up in the moment... this really didn't happen." His uncle recalled him asking Hinn's team why it hadn't worked and being told, "I think God's just taking a break." The ministry later set up a $15,000-plus account in his name for education and health expenses.
  • Jordie Gibson — a 21-year-old from Calgary on kidney dialysis, stopped dialysis on faith and flew to an Anaheim crusade after hearing Hinn preach that people are only cured when they "step out in faith." He was not cured, and had to resume dialysis after returning home.
  • Evander Holyfield — the boxer publicly credited Hinn in 1994 with healing a heart condition. In his own 2008 memoir, he walked the claim back: "No, Hinn didn't heal me. God healed me, working through Hinn" — a meaningfully different claim than the one that made headlines at the time.
  • Raymond Scott — the one case, per the LA Times investigation, with any independent medical documentation among the ministry's claimed thousands of healings.

The investigation that's never happened

It's worth naming a real gap directly. Kathryn Kuhlman — whose services Hinn attended and whose model he built his own ministry on, covered on this site's timeline — was the subject of physician William Nolen's rigorous, name-by-name medical follow-up in the 1970s, which found no verified cures among 23 people she'd publicly claimed to heal. Peter Popoff was the subject of James Randi's forensic exposure of the hidden earpiece feeding him audience information. No comparable book-length, methodical, physician-led investigation has ever been conducted specifically on Hinn's healing claims — despite his being arguably the most visible living faith healer in that same tradition. What exists instead is a consistent pattern across five separate outlets over more than two decades, each using a different method, each reaching the same result. That's not a substitute for the single rigorous study that's never been done — but it's not nothing, either.

What Hinn himself has said when asked directly

Confronted about the absence of verification, Hinn's own on-record answer has been to disclaim responsibility for checking at all: "God never called me to verify. I'm not a doctor." Asked about his own ministry's attempt to build a verification department: "It was chaotic. It was a mess... staff would call and people would be mad," adding that doctors typically refused to cooperate. Asked specifically about cases like Vandenkolk's, where a claimed healing demonstrably didn't happen: "These are things that I cannot explain because I am not the healer. I am human like you. I make mistakes like anybody else," and, of people who falsely claimed healing more generally, that they "mentally convince themselves." That last line does real work — it locates the failure in the crowd's psychology, never in the format that produces it night after night.

Why it looks like something is happening anyway

Skeptical investigator Joe Nickell, a former stage magician who attended a Hinn crusade for the Center for Inquiry's Skeptical Inquirer in 2002, analyzed the "slain in the Spirit" phenomenon — people falling backward en masse when touched — as consistent with hypnotic-style suggestion and social role-expectation rather than any physiological event: "they behave just as if 'hypnotized.'" Beyond that specific mechanism, the standard explanations for apparent crusade healings generally are well documented and don't require anyone to be lying: spontaneous remission (some conditions improve or go into remission for reasons medicine doesn't fully explain, with or without a crusade); misdiagnosis (some "healed" conditions were never what they were claimed to be); the post-hoc fallacy (crediting a healing to a prayer simply because improvement followed it in time); and what's sometimes called subjective validation — a system that "can't lose," where any apparent improvement gets credited to the healer and every failure gets attributed to the sick person's insufficient faith. In a stadium of ten thousand people, base rates alone guarantee some fraction will report feeling better on a given night, for reasons that have nothing to do with what's happening on stage.
