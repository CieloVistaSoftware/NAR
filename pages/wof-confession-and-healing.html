Will Word of Faith Adherents Lie About Healing?

Answering issue #2: yes, often — and the doctrine is built to make them

This article started as a direct answer to a reader question: "A physical healing does not have to be manifest in the here and now. But the person claiming a healing may claim it worked anyway. Is this true? Why do they do that?" Short answer: yes, it's true, it's documented, and it's not really lying in the way that word usually implies — it's closer to a doctrine that removes the option to say "I'm still sick" without that statement itself being treated as the reason you're still sick.

The doctrine is explicit about this, in its own words

This isn't an inference about Word of Faith teaching — it's what the teaching actually says, in its own current, official material. Kenneth Copeland Ministries' own published prayer script instructs believers: "I'm not moved by what I see, feel or experience... Though I haven't yet seen the physical results, I know I have them and am unmoved by negative feelings." Read that carefully: it doesn't say wait for evidence and then confess healing. It says the presence or absence of evidence is supposed to be irrelevant to what you say out loud.

Kenneth Hagin — covered in depth in this site's article on him — taught the same thing more bluntly: "Don't confess your doubts or your fears. This is not to say that you should not admit doubt and fear if you have them. Just don't say anything about it." And: "When you confess your doubts and fears, your weaknesses and diseases, you are openly confessing that the Word of God is not true." The doctrine's proof-texts are Mark 11:23–24 ("what things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them") and Romans 4:17 ("calleth those things which be not as though they were") — the second of which, in its actual context, is Paul describing God's own faithfulness regarding Abraham and Sarah's promised child, not a formula about human speech creating outcomes. Applying it to "declare your tumor gone before any scan says so" is a doctrinal leap the text itself doesn't make.

Why people actually do it

The most credible account of the mechanism comes from someone with both academic standing and direct personal experience of it: Kate Bowler, a Duke Divinity historian who wrote the standard academic history of the prosperity gospel, and who was later diagnosed with stage IV colon cancer while still researching the movement. Her own summary of the doctrine's internal logic: "In a spiritual world in which healing is a divine right, illness is a symptom of unconfessed sin." She has described being told, as a cancer patient, that she simply needed more faith. That's the actual mechanism — not that individual believers are unusually dishonest, but that the belief system makes admitting continued illness equivalent to admitting spiritual failure, in front of a community that will treat that admission as evidence against you. Saying "I'm healed" isn't reporting a fact. It's performing membership in good standing.

Costi Hinn — Benny Hinn's own nephew, raised inside the family ministry before leaving it, mentioned in this site's deeper Benny Hinn coverage — documents this from the inside in his book God, Greed, and the (Prosperity) Gospel: crowds at his uncle's crusades were told everyone present would be healed if they gave, while only some attendees were actually declared healed on stage — and his own mother avoided medical treatment for a tumor for four years before finally having surgery. A separate former Word of Faith adherent, describing her own experience leaving the movement, wrote about learning "the difference between a positive confession and a truthful one" — that she had to consciously give herself permission to admit she was actually sick before she could honestly pray about it at all. That's a genuinely strange thing to have to relearn, and it's exactly what the doctrine trains out of people.

What it looks like when it doesn't work — on the record

William Vandenkolk was 9 years old and legally blind when Benny Hinn declared him healed on stage at a 2001 Las Vegas crusade. ABC's Nightline followed up when he was 17: still legally blind. His own words, years later: "I got caught up in the moment," and "this really didn't happen." His guardian recalled him saying, when asked later if his vision had improved, "I think God's just taking a break." That's about as clear a documented answer to "why do they claim it worked anyway" as exists — a child caught up in a stadium moment, performing what everyone around him expected to see.

HBO's 2001 documentary A Question of Miracles followed seven people who'd claimed healing at Hinn crusades for a year afterward; director Antony Thomas said they found no case where anyone had actually been healed. Gloria and Kenneth Copeland publicly claimed in 2018, and repeated into the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, that believers already have a "flu shot" from Jesus and that Kenneth Copeland "would never have COVID-19 or any other sickness" — a guaranteed-immunity claim stated flatly, in public, on the record, regardless of what actually happened to anyone who believed it. Televangelist W.V. Grant was caught by ABC's Prime Time Live in 1991 staging healings with cold-reading techniques, including manipulating a woman's shoe to make her leg appear to visibly "grow," and using a cane taken from an audience member to stage a dramatic "crippled man walks" moment — the man later said he'd never had trouble walking in the first place.

What critique exists — and what doesn't

Most of the direct, named critique of "positive confession" specifically comes from outside the charismatic movement entirely — cessationist apologists like Hank Hanegraaff, who has written extensively against Word of Faith theology and is cited throughout this site's Kenneth Copeland and Kenneth Hagin articles. This site looked specifically for a still-practicing charismatic or Pentecostal leader — someone who still affirms the ongoing gifts of the Spirit, not a cessationist critic — publicly condemning positive confession by name for pressuring sick people into performing wellness they don't feel, and did not find a clearly sourced example. That gap is itself worth noting: the critique that would carry the most internal weight, from someone the movement can't dismiss as an outsider, appears to be the one that mostly hasn't been made publicly.
