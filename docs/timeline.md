# Timeline

A rough chronology connecting the Shepherding movement to the New Apostolic
Reformation. Dates for organizational milestones are approximate where sources
don't agree precisely; see [Sources](/public/doc-viewer.html?file=docs%2Fsources.md) and [People](/public/doc-viewer.html?file=docs%2Fpeople.md) for
the underlying references.

- **1946–1976** — Kathryn Kuhlman (1907–1976) builds the template later
  televangelist healing services would follow: large auditorium crusades, a
  weekly national TV program (*I Believe in Miracles*), and people testifying
  on stage to being healed, all under a doctrine that the Holy Spirit — not
  Kuhlman herself — performed the cure and she merely discerned it happening.
  She's widely credited with popularizing being "slain in the Spirit." In
  1973, physician and author William A. Nolen, MD followed up on 23 people who
  had publicly claimed healing at one of her Philadelphia services and found
  no verified cures; Nolen also documented a woman who, told at a Kuhlman
  service she was cured of spinal cancer, threw away her back brace and ran
  across the stage — her spine collapsed the next day, and she died four
  months later. Benny Hinn has repeatedly named Kuhlman as the formative
  influence on his own healing ministry. The Kathryn Kuhlman Foundation ceased
  operations in 2016.
- **1952–1983** — Rex Humbard (1919–2007) builds the first weekly nationwide
  televangelism program in the U.S., broadcasting a gospel-of-love message —
  healing through prayer and anointing with oil, deliberately non-political and
  non-doctrinal on air — from the Cathedral of Tomorrow, a purpose-built,
  5,400-seat TV studio-church in Cuyahoga Falls, Ohio. At its peak the broadcast
  reached an estimated 20 million viewers worldwide. Between 1959 and 1973 the
  ministry sold roughly $12 million in unregistered securities through unlicensed
  salesmen, funding assets unrelated to ministry (a Brooklyn girdle factory, an
  office complex, an ad agency); Ohio and the SEC intervened in 1971, forcing
  divestment and a court-mandated ministry budget. Humbard was pushed out of
  Cathedral of Tomorrow leadership in 1982; the complex was sold to fellow
  televangelist Ernest Angley in 1984. No documented direct link to the New
  Apostolic Reformation was found — his significance here is as an early
  cautionary case in televangelism finance, not a doctrinal ancestor.
- **1947–2009** — Oral Roberts (1918–2009) becomes one of the most influential
  radio and television faith healers of the century, and one of the first major
  popularizers — though a recent academic biography cautions not the sole
  originator — of the "seed faith" doctrine: give generously to God's work and
  expect material blessing in return, the direct ancestor of the modern
  prosperity gospel. He founded Oral Roberts University (1963) and, following a
  claimed 1977 vision, the City of Faith Medical and Research Center (opened
  1981). In 1987 he told his television audience he needed to raise $8 million by
  March or "God would call him home" — a widely mocked appeal that nonetheless
  raised over $9 million; City of Faith continued losing over $10 million a year
  and closed in 1989. Roberts wrote the foreword to Bill Hamon's *Prophets and
  Personal Prophecy* — Hamon's Christian International Ministries is a
  foundational apostolic-prophetic network that fed directly into the New
  Apostolic Reformation, making Roberts a documented doctrinal bridge between
  mid-century healing revivalism and the movement this site critiques.
- **1960–2023** — Pat Robertson (1930–2023) founds the Christian Broadcasting
  Network (CBN) in Virginia Beach, launching *The 700 Club* — one of the
  longest continuously-running programs in broadcast history — mixing news
  commentary with on-air "words of knowledge": Robertson naming specific
  ailments (a lump in a caller's breast, a back condition) he claimed God was
  revealing and healing in viewers he had no information about. He ran for
  U.S. president in 1988, and made a string of public predictions that did not
  come to pass: prayer diverting Hurricane Gloria (1985) and later Hurricane
  Florence (2018); a "mass killing" terror attack he said God told him would
  hit the U.S. after September 2007; and, days before the 2012 election,
  relaying that "the Lord told me" Mitt Romney would win and serve two terms —
  Robertson later admitted on air, "I thought I heard from God... what
  happened?" After the January 2010 Haiti earthquake he said Haitians had
  "sworn a pact to the Devil" for independence from France, drawing White
  House condemnation. His son Gordon Robertson has led CBN as president and
  CEO since 2007.
- **1973–present** — Paul and Jan Crouch found Trinity Broadcasting Network
  (TBN) in Southern California; it grows into the world's largest religious
  broadcaster, historically the flagship carrier for Word of Faith/prosperity
  programming (Kenneth Copeland, Benny Hinn, Creflo Dollar, Paula White, and
  others). Paul Crouch died in 2013; Jan Crouch in 2016. TBN's finances and
  conduct have drawn serious documented allegations from within the family
  itself: granddaughter Brittany Koper's 2012 lawsuit alleged tens of millions
  of ministry dollars were diverted to Crouch-family homes, jets, and personal
  expenses, and that she was fired and threatened with a firearm after
  refusing to help; separately, granddaughter Carra Crouch sued alleging she
  was raped by a TBN employee at 13 and that Jan Crouch pressured her not to
  report it — a 2017 jury found Jan Crouch and TBN's parent corporation liable
  and awarded $2 million in damages. Paul and Jan's son Matt Crouch is TBN's
  current president and chairman.
- **1934–2003** — Kenneth Hagin, widely credited as the father of the Word
  of Faith movement, builds his teaching around a healing testimony from
  his own youth and founds RHEMA Bible Training College (1974), the
  training ground behind much of the modern prosperity-gospel landscape.
  His signature doctrine — that believers are "the same class of being"
  as God, "an incarnation" the same way Christ was — comes with a
  documented text-critical problem: a 1984 academic study found his 1967
  booklet closely paralleled a 1932 work by John A. MacMillan, and D.R.
  McConnell's standard critical history, *A Different Gospel* (1988),
  documents Hagin copying "word-for-word without documentation" from at
  least eight of E.W. Kenyon's books. Hagin's own defense: the overlap was
  "the same Spirit... leading and directing." He also taught that Christ's
  spirit went to hell and was tormented by Satan for three days before
  being "born again" there — a doctrine a 2007 University of Edinburgh
  thesis traces directly from Kenyon through Hagin to Copeland, and finds
  incompatible with the historic Christian understanding of the
  incarnation and atonement. He died of a sudden cardiac event in 2003,
  with no account found of his own ministry reckoning with what that means
  for a doctrine built on guaranteed healing. Full account in
  [Kenneth Hagin](?page=kenneth-hagin).
- **1967–present** — Kenneth Copeland (b. 1936), a protégé of Word of Faith
  founder Kenneth Hagin, builds Kenneth Copeland Ministries into one of the
  most durable teachers of "positive confession": that healing and
  prosperity are guaranteed to any believer with sufficient faith, and that
  failure to receive them reflects the believer's deficiency, not the
  doctrine's. On *Larry King Live*, asked why healing sometimes doesn't
  happen, Copeland said "the receiving is on our part... if we are not
  receiving, then there is a misconnection" — placing the failure
  permanently on the believer's side. A 2013 measles outbreak at his own
  Eagle Mountain International Church, tied directly to congregants
  declining vaccination on faith grounds, and a woman's 2004 cancer death
  after refusing treatment specifically because of Copeland's television
  broadcasts, are both documented. Copeland himself has preached he would
  "never be sick another day" in his life, then revealed a pacemaker in
  2022; he has publicly predicted he'll live to 120. A three-year U.S.
  Senate Finance Committee investigation (2007–2011) found Copeland stating
  on an internal video that he is personally "a billionaire," with the
  ministry receiving an estimated $80–90 million a year and Copeland
  holding unilateral veto power over his own church's board — the
  investigation closed with no penalties, not because nothing troubling
  was found, but because Committee staff said witnesses were too afraid of
  retaliation to testify. Full account in
  [Kenneth Copeland & "Enough Faith to Be Healed"](?page=kenneth-copeland).
- **1983–present** — Benny Hinn (b. 1952, Jaffa, Israel) becomes the most
  visible living faith healer in the Kuhlman tradition. He attended a Kuhlman
  service in Pittsburgh in 1973 and later wrote a book crediting her as his
  formative influence; founded Orlando Christian Center in 1983 and launched
  *This Is Your Day* on TBN in 1990, building his ministry around mass
  "Miracle Crusades" where attendees are "slain in the Spirit" — falling
  backward, often caught by staff positioned for exactly that. He has made a
  string of specific claims that drew wide ridicule and correction: on TBN in
  1990 he taught the Trinity was actually nine persons, each with his own
  "spirit, soul, and body," calling it "a very dumb statement" and retracting
  it within the week once challenged; in December 1991 he taught that before
  the Fall, Adam could fly, "even to the moon"; in 1999 he claimed a vision
  that TV viewers who placed a deceased loved one's hand on their television
  screen during his broadcast would see them resurrected. Multiple
  investigations followed up on his healing claims and found the same pattern
  documented elsewhere in this timeline: HBO's *A Question of Miracles*
  (2001) tracked seven claimed healings for a year and found none held up;
  the CBC's *the fifth estate* (2004), using hidden cameras, filmed staff
  actively steering the most visibly disabled attendees away from the stage
  and found previously "healed" individuals still sick or dead. Financially,
  Benny Hinn Ministries was raided by IRS criminal investigators and U.S.
  Postal Inspectors in April 2017 over suspected tax evasion (no charges are
  documented as having followed), and the ministry has faced repeated
  creditor judgments and wage garnishments into 2026; MinistryWatch gives it
  a financial transparency grade of F. In September 2019 Hinn publicly
  renounced prosperity-gospel "seed" fundraising on air — "giving has become
  such a gimmick... it's making me sick to my stomach" — but was recorded
  soliciting seed donations again within weeks, echoing an identical
  reversal after his 1990 Trinity retraction. His own nephew, Costi Hinn,
  left the family ministry and published *God, Greed, and the (Prosperity)
  Gospel* (2019), calling prosperity teaching "the most hateful and abusive
  kind of false teaching plaguing the church today." A much deeper look at
  the healing-claim investigations specifically — five separate
  investigative teams across three countries and 25 years, all reaching
  the same conclusion — is in
  [Benny Hinn: Has He Ever Healed Anyone?](?page=benny-hinn-healings).
- **1997–present** — Marcus and Joni Lamb launch Daystar Television Network
  out of Bedford, Texas, funded in part by Kenneth Copeland Ministries and
  built around the same prosperity/Word of Faith programming roster as TBN
  (Copeland, Creflo Dollar, Joel Osteen, Andrew Wommack). In late 2010, the
  Lambs disclosed on their own morning show that Marcus had had an affair,
  saying they were going public rather than pay $7.5 million demanded by
  people threatening to expose it. Marcus Lamb died of COVID-19 in November
  2021, after having promoted unproven COVID treatments on Daystar
  programming; Joni Lamb continued leading the network until her own death in
  May 2026, after which leadership passed to Daystar's board amid a
  publicly contested family succession dispute.
- **1985–present** — Perry Stone (b. 1959) founds Voice of Evangelism, later
  hosting *Manna-Fest* on TBN from 2000, teaching heavily on end-times Bible
  prophecy. He has made a series of public claims that did not hold up:
  in December 2015 he said ISIS had planted sleeper cells and weapons caches
  in warehouses across all 50 U.S. states, a claim no law-enforcement or
  intelligence source ever corroborated; in 2018 he claimed to possess
  classified information about a living Watergate conspirator and knowledge of
  "64,000 emails" proving Democratic-Russia collusion, and said an unnamed
  billionaire told him world leaders were secret "Luciferians"; in March 2020
  he characterized COVID-19 as divine punishment for abortion and same-sex
  marriage. Separately, in July 2020 and again more extensively in December
  2021, Chattanooga Times Free Press reporting detailed sexual-misconduct
  allegations from eleven people against Stone; a Bradley County, Tennessee
  grand jury declined to indict him in April 2023 after review by the
  Tennessee Bureau of Investigation, and he denies wrongdoing.
- **The healing claims, examined** — Across decades of on-air healing
  ministry — Kuhlman, Roberts, Robertson, and the wider circuit these networks
  carried — no rigorously documented, independently verified miraculous
  healing has ever been produced, despite investigators specifically looking
  for one. James Randi's book-length investigation *The Faith Healers* (1987)
  found none: 2,000 leaflets distributed after a W.V. Grant crusade asking
  anyone actually healed to come forward drew zero replies, and Oral Roberts's
  own submitted "evidence" cases either lacked a verifiable prior diagnosis or
  the patient had simply had conventional surgery anyway. The clearest
  documented mechanism behind "word of knowledge" healing claims is Randi's
  1986 exposure of televangelist Peter Popoff, who was proven to be receiving
  audience members' names and ailments through a hidden earpiece, radioed to
  him live by his wife from prayer-request cards collected before the
  service — not supernatural revelation, but a fed earpiece, aired publicly on
  *The Tonight Show*. For comparison: even the Catholic Church's own shrine at
  Lourdes, which has recorded roughly 30,000 self-reported healings over its
  history, has formally recognized only 64 as medically inexplicable after
  its own physician-led review — a rigor that on-air healing segments on these
  networks have never been subjected to.
- **Why people claim it worked anyway** — Word of Faith doctrine doesn't
  just fail to produce verified healings; it's built with an explicit
  mechanism for what happens when a claimed healing doesn't hold up.
  Kenneth Copeland Ministries' own published prayer scripts instruct
  believers: "I'm not moved by what I see, feel or experience... though I
  haven't yet seen the physical results, I know I have them." Duke
  historian Kate Bowler, later diagnosed with stage IV cancer while
  researching the movement academically, summarized the effect plainly:
  "In a spiritual world in which healing is a divine right, illness is a
  symptom of unconfessed sin." William Vandenkolk, declared healed of
  blindness on stage by Benny Hinn at age 9, was found still legally blind
  years later by ABC's *Nightline* — his own account: "I got caught up in
  the moment... this really didn't happen." Full account, answering a
  reader's question, in
  [Will Word of Faith Adherents Lie About Healing?](?page=wof-confession-and-healing).
- **Beyond healing claims** — The pattern above isn't limited to healing
  claims specifically. The wider charismatic movement's habit of vesting
  enormous, unaccountable authority in a single leader has produced a long
  record of financial fraud, sexual abuse, and cover-ups, spanning decades
  and continuing into the present:
- **1960s–1984** — Hobart Freeman founds Faith Assembly in Wilmot, Indiana,
  teaching that medications are "demonic" and requiring members to undergo
  ritual "deliverance" from any medicine they'd ever taken. A peer-reviewed
  1998 study found Faith Assembly responsible for 64 documented child
  deaths, 1975–1995 — the highest count of any religious group studied —
  and contemporaneous journalism put the broader total, including adults,
  as high as 80–91. Freeman was himself indicted for reckless homicide in
  1984 and died before trial, of a condition — untreated diabetes, by one
  account — his own teaching forbade him from treating. Full account in
  [Hobart Freeman & Faith Assembly](?page=hobart-freeman).
- **1987–1988** — Jim Bakker resigns from PTL after a hush-money payment to
  Jessica Hahn comes to light, and is later convicted on 24 counts of mail
  and wire fraud for overselling Heritage USA "lifetime partnerships." Six
  months later, Jimmy Swaggart delivers a televised "I have sinned"
  confession after being photographed with a prostitute, is defrocked by the
  Assemblies of God for refusing to complete his suspension, and — after a
  second, nearly identical scandal in 1991 — tells his congregation "the
  Lord told me it's flat none of your business." Full account, including the
  fraud scheme's mechanics and what happened to both men afterward, in
  [Jim Bakker & Jimmy Swaggart](?page=bakker-swaggart).
- **2006** — Ted Haggard, president of the National Association of
  Evangelicals and founder of New Life Church, resigns after a male escort
  publicly alleges a three-year sexual relationship and drug purchases. His
  church-run "restoration" process has no actual enforcement power: he ends
  it unilaterally in 2008, launches an independent church in 2010, and faces
  nearly identical allegations again in 2022 — refusing to step down either
  time. Full account in [Ted Haggard](?page=ted-haggard).
- **2008** — At the height of the Lakeland Revival, NAR founder C. Peter
  Wagner personally leads a public ceremony commissioning Todd Bentley,
  declaring his "authority will increase, your favor will increase." Weeks
  later Bentley's marriage collapses amid an affair with a staff member; a
  hasty restoration process readmits him to ministry within about a year,
  and a second, more serious 2019 disqualification for a pattern of sexual
  misconduct still didn't stop him from being invited back to preach at a
  major NAR-adjacent conference in 2024. Full account in
  [Todd Bentley & the Lakeland Revival](?page=todd-bentley-lakeland).
- **2008–2017** — Federal agents raid Tony Alamo's Arkansas compound,
  removing six girls aged 10–17; he's convicted of transporting minors
  across state lines for sex, with trial testimony describing him "marrying"
  girls as young as 8 and 9. A 2014 Arkansas court awards his surviving
  victims a $525 million judgment — reportedly the largest personal-injury
  award in the state's history. Full account in [Tony Alamo](?page=tony-alamo).
- **2010–2017** — Bishop Eddie Long, whose 25,000-member New Birth
  Missionary Baptist Church made him one of the country's most prominent
  Black megachurch pastors, settles civil lawsuits from five young men who
  say he used his role as their spiritual mentor to coerce them into sexual
  relationships as teenagers. He never admits wrongdoing and remains pastor
  until his 2017 death. Full account in [Bishop Eddie Long & New Birth](?page=eddie-long).
- **2020–2023** — Hillsong Church, whose worship music shaped charismatic
  Christianity globally, comes apart: Carl Lentz is fired from Hillsong NYC
  after an affair, followed by a leaked internal report documenting a
  broader pattern of misconduct; founder Brian Houston is charged with (and
  later acquitted of) concealing his father's child sex abuse, then
  separately resigns over unrelated personal conduct. The U.S. network loses
  most of its campuses to independence in the years that follow. Full
  account in [Hillsong: Brian Houston & Carl Lentz](?page=hillsong).
- **2023–2025** — Mike Bickle, founder of the International House of Prayer
  Kansas City and a charter member of C. Peter Wagner's own Apostolic
  Council of Prophetic Elders, is accused by multiple women of decades of
  sexual abuse. An independent investigation funded by an outside
  organization — IHOP-KC itself never fully endorsed the findings —
  concludes he abused 17 survivors and that leadership used internal
  "accountability" meetings to silence victims rather than investigate him.
  Full account, including direct commentary connecting this case to NAR's
  apostolic governance model, in [Mike Bickle & IHOP-KC](?page=mike-bickle-ihopkc).
- **2019–2025** — Charismatic apologist Michael Brown organizes and oversees
  the accountability process that gets Todd Bentley declared "disqualified
  from ministry" in January 2020 — a genuine, if imperfectly enforced, case
  of the movement policing its own. Brown also defends several NAR-adjacent
  leaders by name and later mediates, unsuccessfully, in the Mike Bickle
  scandal above. In November 2024, Brown himself steps back from ministry
  after misconduct allegations surface; an independent investigator's April
  2025 report calls it "sexually abusive misconduct" against two women — a
  hand-picked elder team then overrides that finding, calls it "moral
  indiscretions," and recommends his return to ministry. Full account in
  [Michael Brown](?page=michael-brown).
- **2024–2026** — Robert Morris resigns as senior pastor of Gateway Church,
  one of the country's largest megachurches, after Cindy Clemishire
  publicly says he abused her starting at age 12 in 1982. Unlike most cases
  on this site, this one reaches an actual criminal conviction: an Oklahoma
  grand jury indicts him in 2025, he pleads guilty to five felony counts,
  and is sentenced to prison time followed by years of probation. Full
  account in [Robert Morris & Gateway Church](?page=robert-morris-gateway).
- **1970s** — The Shepherding (Discipleship) movement takes shape around Derek
  Prince, Bob Mumford, Don Basham, Charles Simpson, and Ern Baxter (the "Fort
  Lauderdale" teachers), teaching direct personal submission to an assigned
  "shepherd."
- **1983** — Derek Prince breaks away first, privately, in a letter to friends and
  colleagues rather than a public statement.
- **1986** — Christian Growth Ministries, the Fort Lauderdale organization behind
  the movement, dissolves. Mumford, Prince, Basham, and Baxter release their
  disciples from the formal "covenant relationships" — three years before Mumford's
  public apology below.
- **March 1989** — Don Basham dies, before any public reckoning with the movement's
  errors takes place.
- **November 1989** — Bob Mumford, not the movement's leaders jointly, delivers a
  public statement of repentance at a Christian Believers United conference,
  reportedly in Ridgecrest, North Carolina: "I repent. I was wrong. I ask for
  forgiveness." Charles Simpson explicitly defends the movement and does not
  apologize; Ern Baxter, teaching in Australia, declines to comment and never
  publicly repudiates the teaching before his 1993 death. Full detail, with
  sourcing, is in [Don Basham & Charles Simpson](?page=don-basham-charles-simpson).
- **1990s–2000s** — C. Peter Wagner, drawing on trends he observed in global
  Pentecostal/charismatic Christianity, begins using and promoting the term "New
  Apostolic Reformation" to describe networks led by figures he identifies as
  functioning apostles and prophets.
- **2000s–2010s** — Apostolic networks and ministries associated with the term grow:
  Generals International, Harvest International Ministry, Bethel Church's media and
  training arms, TheCall's mass prayer gatherings, and others.
- **2016** — C. Peter Wagner dies; leadership of his institutional legacy passes to
  Ché Ahn.
- **January 6, 2021** — Journalists and researchers, most thoroughly Matthew D.
  Taylor, document the presence of NAR-associated imagery, leaders, and prophetic
  rhetoric at the U.S. Capitol events, drawing wider public and political attention
  to the movement's Seven Mountain Mandate teaching.
- **Ongoing** — This site is part of that ongoing public conversation, written from
  firsthand experience with the wider charismatic movement. See the
  [Introduction](?page=introduction) for that background, and the
  [main article](?page=nar) for the current state of the theological argument.
