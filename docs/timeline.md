# Timeline

A rough chronology connecting the Shepherding movement to the New Apostolic
Reformation. Dates for organizational milestones are approximate where sources
don't agree precisely; see [Sources](/public/doc-viewer.html?file=docs%2Fsources.md) and [People](/public/doc-viewer.html?file=docs%2Fpeople.md) for
the underlying references.

- **1946–1976** — Kathryn Kuhlman (1907–1976) builds the template later
  televangelist healing services would follow: large auditorium crusades, a
  weekly national TV program (*I Believe in Miracles*), and people testifying
  on stage to being healed, all under a doctrine that the Holy Spirit — not
  Kuhlman herself — performed the cure and she merely discerned it happening.
  She's widely credited with popularizing being "slain in the Spirit." In
  1973, physician and author William A. Nolen, MD followed up on 23 people who
  had publicly claimed healing at one of her Philadelphia services and found
  no verified cures; Nolen also documented a woman who, told at a Kuhlman
  service she was cured of spinal cancer, threw away her back brace and ran
  across the stage — her spine collapsed the next day, and she died four
  months later. Benny Hinn has repeatedly named Kuhlman as the formative
  influence on his own healing ministry. The Kathryn Kuhlman Foundation ceased
  operations in 2016.
- **1952–1983** — Rex Humbard (1919–2007) builds the first weekly nationwide
  televangelism program in the U.S., broadcasting a gospel-of-love message —
  healing through prayer and anointing with oil, deliberately non-political and
  non-doctrinal on air — from the Cathedral of Tomorrow, a purpose-built,
  5,400-seat TV studio-church in Cuyahoga Falls, Ohio. At its peak the broadcast
  reached an estimated 20 million viewers worldwide. Between 1959 and 1973 the
  ministry sold roughly $12 million in unregistered securities through unlicensed
  salesmen, funding assets unrelated to ministry (a Brooklyn girdle factory, an
  office complex, an ad agency); Ohio and the SEC intervened in 1971, forcing
  divestment and a court-mandated ministry budget. Humbard was pushed out of
  Cathedral of Tomorrow leadership in 1982; the complex was sold to fellow
  televangelist Ernest Angley in 1984. No documented direct link to the New
  Apostolic Reformation was found — his significance here is as an early
  cautionary case in televangelism finance, not a doctrinal ancestor.
- **1947–2009** — Oral Roberts (1918–2009) becomes one of the most influential
  radio and television faith healers of the century, and one of the first major
  popularizers — though a recent academic biography cautions not the sole
  originator — of the "seed faith" doctrine: give generously to God's work and
  expect material blessing in return, the direct ancestor of the modern
  prosperity gospel. He founded Oral Roberts University (1963) and, following a
  claimed 1977 vision, the City of Faith Medical and Research Center (opened
  1981). In 1987 he told his television audience he needed to raise $8 million by
  March or "God would call him home" — a widely mocked appeal that nonetheless
  raised over $9 million; City of Faith continued losing over $10 million a year
  and closed in 1989. Roberts wrote the foreword to Bill Hamon's *Prophets and
  Personal Prophecy* — Hamon's Christian International Ministries is a
  foundational apostolic-prophetic network that fed directly into the New
  Apostolic Reformation, making Roberts a documented doctrinal bridge between
  mid-century healing revivalism and the movement this site critiques.
- **1960–2023** — Pat Robertson (1930–2023) founds the Christian Broadcasting
  Network (CBN) in Virginia Beach, launching *The 700 Club* — one of the
  longest continuously-running programs in broadcast history — mixing news
  commentary with on-air "words of knowledge": Robertson naming specific
  ailments (a lump in a caller's breast, a back condition) he claimed God was
  revealing and healing in viewers he had no information about. He ran for
  U.S. president in 1988, and made a string of public predictions that did not
  come to pass: prayer diverting Hurricane Gloria (1985) and later Hurricane
  Florence (2018); a "mass killing" terror attack he said God told him would
  hit the U.S. after September 2007; and, days before the 2012 election,
  relaying that "the Lord told me" Mitt Romney would win and serve two terms —
  Robertson later admitted on air, "I thought I heard from God... what
  happened?" After the January 2010 Haiti earthquake he said Haitians had
  "sworn a pact to the Devil" for independence from France, drawing White
  House condemnation. His son Gordon Robertson has led CBN as president and
  CEO since 2007.
- **1973–present** — Paul and Jan Crouch found Trinity Broadcasting Network
  (TBN) in Southern California; it grows into the world's largest religious
  broadcaster, historically the flagship carrier for Word of Faith/prosperity
  programming (Kenneth Copeland, Benny Hinn, Creflo Dollar, Paula White, and
  others). Paul Crouch died in 2013; Jan Crouch in 2016. TBN's finances and
  conduct have drawn serious documented allegations from within the family
  itself: granddaughter Brittany Koper's 2012 lawsuit alleged tens of millions
  of ministry dollars were diverted to Crouch-family homes, jets, and personal
  expenses, and that she was fired and threatened with a firearm after
  refusing to help; separately, granddaughter Carra Crouch sued alleging she
  was raped by a TBN employee at 13 and that Jan Crouch pressured her not to
  report it — a 2017 jury found Jan Crouch and TBN's parent corporation liable
  and awarded $2 million in damages. Paul and Jan's son Matt Crouch is TBN's
  current president and chairman.
- **1997–present** — Marcus and Joni Lamb launch Daystar Television Network
  out of Bedford, Texas, funded in part by Kenneth Copeland Ministries and
  built around the same prosperity/Word of Faith programming roster as TBN
  (Copeland, Creflo Dollar, Joel Osteen, Andrew Wommack). In late 2010, the
  Lambs disclosed on their own morning show that Marcus had had an affair,
  saying they were going public rather than pay $7.5 million demanded by
  people threatening to expose it. Marcus Lamb died of COVID-19 in November
  2021, after having promoted unproven COVID treatments on Daystar
  programming; Joni Lamb continued leading the network until her own death in
  May 2026, after which leadership passed to Daystar's board amid a
  publicly contested family succession dispute.
- **1985–present** — Perry Stone (b. 1959) founds Voice of Evangelism, later
  hosting *Manna-Fest* on TBN from 2000, teaching heavily on end-times Bible
  prophecy. He has made a series of public claims that did not hold up:
  in December 2015 he said ISIS had planted sleeper cells and weapons caches
  in warehouses across all 50 U.S. states, a claim no law-enforcement or
  intelligence source ever corroborated; in 2018 he claimed to possess
  classified information about a living Watergate conspirator and knowledge of
  "64,000 emails" proving Democratic-Russia collusion, and said an unnamed
  billionaire told him world leaders were secret "Luciferians"; in March 2020
  he characterized COVID-19 as divine punishment for abortion and same-sex
  marriage. Separately, in July 2020 and again more extensively in December
  2021, Chattanooga Times Free Press reporting detailed sexual-misconduct
  allegations from eleven people against Stone; a Bradley County, Tennessee
  grand jury declined to indict him in April 2023 after review by the
  Tennessee Bureau of Investigation, and he denies wrongdoing.
- **The healing claims, examined** — Across decades of on-air healing
  ministry — Kuhlman, Roberts, Robertson, and the wider circuit these networks
  carried — no rigorously documented, independently verified miraculous
  healing has ever been produced, despite investigators specifically looking
  for one. James Randi's book-length investigation *The Faith Healers* (1987)
  found none: 2,000 leaflets distributed after a W.V. Grant crusade asking
  anyone actually healed to come forward drew zero replies, and Oral Roberts's
  own submitted "evidence" cases either lacked a verifiable prior diagnosis or
  the patient had simply had conventional surgery anyway. The clearest
  documented mechanism behind "word of knowledge" healing claims is Randi's
  1986 exposure of televangelist Peter Popoff, who was proven to be receiving
  audience members' names and ailments through a hidden earpiece, radioed to
  him live by his wife from prayer-request cards collected before the
  service — not supernatural revelation, but a fed earpiece, aired publicly on
  *The Tonight Show*. For comparison: even the Catholic Church's own shrine at
  Lourdes, which has recorded roughly 30,000 self-reported healings over its
  history, has formally recognized only 64 as medically inexplicable after
  its own physician-led review — a rigor that on-air healing segments on these
  networks have never been subjected to.
- **1970s** — The Shepherding (Discipleship) movement takes shape around Derek
  Prince, Bob Mumford, Don Basham, Charles Simpson, and Ern Baxter (the "Fort
  Lauderdale" teachers), teaching direct personal submission to an assigned
  "shepherd."
- **1983** — Derek Prince breaks away first, privately, in a letter to friends and
  colleagues rather than a public statement.
- **1986** — Christian Growth Ministries, the Fort Lauderdale organization behind
  the movement, dissolves. Mumford, Prince, Basham, and Baxter release their
  disciples from the formal "covenant relationships" — three years before Mumford's
  public apology below.
- **March 1989** — Don Basham dies, before any public reckoning with the movement's
  errors takes place.
- **November 1989** — Bob Mumford, not the movement's leaders jointly, delivers a
  public statement of repentance at a Christian Believers United conference,
  reportedly in Ridgecrest, North Carolina: "I repent. I was wrong. I ask for
  forgiveness." Charles Simpson explicitly defends the movement and does not
  apologize; Ern Baxter, teaching in Australia, declines to comment and never
  publicly repudiates the teaching before his 1993 death. Full detail, with
  sourcing, is in [Don Basham & Charles Simpson](?page=don-basham-charles-simpson).
- **1990s–2000s** — C. Peter Wagner, drawing on trends he observed in global
  Pentecostal/charismatic Christianity, begins using and promoting the term "New
  Apostolic Reformation" to describe networks led by figures he identifies as
  functioning apostles and prophets.
- **2000s–2010s** — Apostolic networks and ministries associated with the term grow:
  Generals International, Harvest International Ministry, Bethel Church's media and
  training arms, TheCall's mass prayer gatherings, and others.
- **2016** — C. Peter Wagner dies; leadership of his institutional legacy passes to
  Ché Ahn.
- **January 6, 2021** — Journalists and researchers, most thoroughly Matthew D.
  Taylor, document the presence of NAR-associated imagery, leaders, and prophetic
  rhetoric at the U.S. Capitol events, drawing wider public and political attention
  to the movement's Seven Mountain Mandate teaching.
- **Ongoing** — This site is part of that ongoing public conversation, written from
  firsthand experience with the wider charismatic movement. See the
  [Introduction](?page=introduction) for that background, and the
  [main article](?page=nar) for the current state of the theological argument.
